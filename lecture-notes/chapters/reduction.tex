\begin{objectives}
\item Recognize claims that need \emph{imported} hardness, and pick the source problem.
\item Match the rung to the currency: decision, ratio, count, time, space.
\item Audit a gadget: instances forward, quality backward, parameters in every regime.
\item Run reductions backwards to certify tractability --- then glue both sides into a dichotomy.
\end{objectives}

Most ``impossible'' statements in database research are not proved from scratch: they are
\emph{imported}. A reduction exhibits a computable translation from a problem already
believed hard --- CLIQUE, Set Cover, inner-product estimation, hyperclique detection ---
into the problem at hand, so that any algorithm, approximation, private mechanism, or
sampler for the target would also solve the source. The translation is a two-way conduit:
instances travel forward through the gadget, and whatever the paper cares about --- running
time, approximation ratio, output count, memory, accuracy --- travels backward, inheriting
the source's hardness. Run in the constructive direction, the same machinery turns new
problems into old ones with known algorithms. Reductions are the most used theoretical
tool in our SIGMOD'26 corpus: 50 of 228 tagged papers rely on them, 32 of those pair the
reduction with an explicit hardness claim, and the heaviest concentration is graph
analytics (14 papers).

This chapter builds the ladder of reduction types first --- each rung named by the
\emph{currency} it preserves --- and walks four corpus exemplars up it;
Section~\ref{sec:red-advanced} adds the advanced rungs (fine-grained hypotheses,
reduction chains, algebraic transfer lemmas) as extension reading.

\section{The tool: reductions}
\label{sec:red-tool}

The problem class is uniform across applications. Somewhere there is a source problem
$H$ that a community has already failed to solve --- CLIQUE, Set Cover, Disjointness,
Hamiltonian path --- together with evidence of its hardness: a proof (NP-completeness),
a widely believed conjecture (the Unique Games Conjecture), or a quantitative hypothesis
(SETH, combinatorial Boolean matrix multiplication, communication lower bounds). The
paper's target problem $P$ is the one the reader actually cares about. What a reduction
supplies is a pair of polynomial-time maps: a \emph{gadget} $f$ that builds an instance
of $P$ out of every instance of $H$, and a \emph{decoder} $g$ that reads a solution of
quality $q$ for $f(x)$ back into a solution of quality $g(q)$ for $x$. Composing the
two, any solver for $P$ of quality $q$ is a solver for $H$ of quality $g(q)$; if $H$'s
hardness statement rules that out, it rules out the solver for $P$.
Figure~\ref{fig:red-skeleton} schematizes the two halves.

\begin{remarkbox}[Hypotheses and currencies]
Throughout, the \emph{source} $H$ carries a hardness statement at some resolution:
qualitative (NP-hardness, \#P-completeness), ratio-quantitative (no $r$-approximation
under the Unique Games Conjecture), or fully quantitative (no $O(n^{2-\varepsilon})$
algorithm under SETH; no $o(m)$-communication protocol for Disjointness). The
\emph{currency} of a reduction is the quantity that travels backward through the
decoder: yes/no membership, objective value (hence approximation ratio), output count,
running time, memory, or estimation error. The target inherits exactly the source's
resolution --- no more: an NP-hardness source cannot yield an inapproximability ratio,
and a conditional source yields a conditional bound.
\end{remarkbox}

\subsection{The ladder}
\label{sec:red-ladder}

The classical rung preserves decision membership
\citep{reduction-bg2,reduction-bg1,reduction-bg3}.

\begin{definition}[Polynomial-time many-one reduction]\label{def:red-manyone}
Let $L_1, L_2$ be decision problems. We write $L_1 \le_m^p L_2$ if there is a
polynomial-time computable function $f$ with $x \in L_1 \iff f(x) \in L_2$ for all $x$.
\eop
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Hardness transfer]\label{prop:red-hardness}
If $L_1$ is NP-hard and $L_1 \le_m^p L_2$, then $L_2$ is NP-hard. \eop
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A polynomial-time algorithm for $L_2$, composed with $f$, decides $L_1$; a polynomial
composition of polynomials is polynomial.
\end{proof}

This rung already reaches into databases, at the very origin of query-evaluation
complexity \citep{reduction-bg6}:

\begin{example}[Conjunctive-query evaluation is NP-complete]\label{ex:red-cq}
Given a graph $G$ and $k$, build in polynomial time the database $D_G$ holding the edge
relation of $G$, and let $Q_k$ be the conjunctive query
\[
Q_k \;=\; \exists v_1 \dots \exists v_k \,.\,
\bigwedge_{1 \le i < j \le k} E(v_i, v_j). \eopm
\]
Then $G$ has a $k$-clique iff $Q_k(D_G) \ne \emptyset$. Since CLIQUE is NP-complete,
CQ evaluation is NP-hard (and it lies in NP).
\end{example}

Database problems, however, usually carry objectives --- cost, error, latency --- so the
reduction must preserve \emph{quality}, not just membership. The standard rung is the
approximation-preserving, or $L$-, reduction \citep{reduction-bg4,reduction-bg1}:

\begin{definition}[$L$-reduction]\label{def:red-lred}
An optimization problem $A$ L-reduces to $B$ if there are polynomial-time maps $f$
(instances) and $g$ (solutions) and constants $\alpha, \beta > 0$ such that, for every
instance $x$ of $A$, writing $c_A, c_B$ for the objective values:
(i)~$\mathrm{OPT}_B(f(x)) \le \alpha \cdot \mathrm{OPT}_A(x)$; and
(ii)~every solution $y$ of $f(x)$ maps to a solution $g(y)$ of $x$ with
$|c_A(g(y)) - \mathrm{OPT}_A(x)| \le \beta \cdot |c_B(y) - \mathrm{OPT}_B(f(x))|$.
\eop
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Ratio transfer]\label{prop:red-ratio}
If $A$ L-reduces to $B$ with constants $\alpha,\beta$ and $B$ admits a polynomial-time
$r$-approximation, then $A$ admits an $\alpha\beta r$-approximation. Hence
inapproximability of $A$ --- e.g.\ Set Cover's $\ln n$ threshold --- transfers to $B$.
\eop
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Apply the $B$-solver to $f(x)$ and decode by $g$: chaining (i) and (ii), the excess of
$c_A(g(y))$ over $\mathrm{OPT}_A(x)$ is at most $\alpha\beta$ times the excess of
$c_B(y)$ over $\mathrm{OPT}_B(f(x))$, so the achieved cost is within a $1 +
\alpha\beta(r-1)$, hence an $\alpha\beta r$, factor of the optimum.
\end{proof}

\sidenote{A \emph{gap} reduction is the same idea with the ratio built into the gadget:
$f$ maps yes-instances to solutions of cost $\le c$ and no-instances to instances where
every solution costs $> \gamma c$, so any $(\gamma-\varepsilon)$-approximation for $B$
would decide $H$. Gap form and $L$-form are interchangeable currency for ratios; the
corpus exemplar of Section~\ref{sec:red-outliers} uses the $L$-form against Set Cover.}

When polynomial versus exponential time is too coarse, the source carries a quantitative
hypothesis, and the reduction must \emph{audit parameters} \citep{reduction-bg5}:

\begin{proposition}[Fine-grained transfer]\label{prop:red-fine}
Suppose a polynomial-time gadget $f$ translates instances of $H$ with parameters $\pi$
into instances of $P$, and a solver for $P$ can be leveraged (with polynomial
postprocessing) using $N(\pi)$ calls on produced instances of size $s(\pi)$. Then any
algorithm for $P$ running in time $T$ on size-$s(\pi)$ inputs yields an algorithm for
$H$ of cost $\operatorname{poly}(\pi) + N(\pi)\cdot T(s(\pi))$. In particular, if this
beats the time lower bound assumed for $H$ \emph{in every parameter regime}, the
translated bound holds for $P$ under the same hypothesis. \eop
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
Count the cost of the emulation; the caveat is the whole point --- a gadget whose
parameter blow-up is benign in one regime and wild in another proves nothing there
(cf.\ Section~\ref{sec:red-continual} for the audit done right).
\end{proof}

Two further rungs complete the ladder. For counting problems the currency is the output
count, and the natural notion is oracle access:

\begin{definition}[Counting (Turing) reduction]\label{def:red-count}
A counting problem $\#A$ reduces to $\#B$, written $\#A \le_\#^T \#B$, if $\#A(x)$ is
computable in polynomial time given oracle access to $\#B$ on instances constructible
in polynomial time. \eop
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[\#P-hardness transfer]\label{prop:red-sharp}
If $\#A$ is \#P-complete and $\#A \le_\#^T \#B$, then $\#B$ is \#P-hard. \eop
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Substitute the oracle: a polynomial-time algorithm computing $\#B$ turns the Turing
reduction into a polynomial-time algorithm for the \#P-complete $\#A$.
\end{proof}

Finally, for streaming algorithms the classical bridge converts space into
communication:

\begin{proposition}[One pass, one message]\label{prop:red-comm}
If a one-pass streaming algorithm with $s$ bits of memory computes a stream function
$F$, then the two-party problem ``Alice holds a stream prefix $x$, Bob holds the
suffix $y$, compute $F(x \cdot y)$'' has a deterministic protocol of communication
$s$. A $t$-pass algorithm gives communication $O(s \cdot t)$. Consequently, any
$\Omega(c)$ communication lower bound for the two-party problem forces
$\Omega(c)$ (resp.\ $\Omega(c/t)$) space for one-pass (resp.\ $t$-pass) streams. \eop
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Alice simulates the algorithm on her prefix and sends Bob the final memory state ---
$s$ bits, from which Bob resumes and finishes the pass. Multiple passes cross the
channel once per pass per direction.
\end{proof}

\subsection{How to choose the rung}
\label{sec:red-choose}

The selection procedure is mechanical once two questions are answered. \emph{First,
what must the claim rule out?} An algorithm (any quality) --- rung of
Definition~\ref{def:red-manyone}; an approximation at some ratio ---
Definition~\ref{def:red-lred} or its gap form; a fast algorithm at a refined running
time --- Proposition~\ref{prop:red-fine} under a quantitative hypothesis; a counter ---
Definition~\ref{def:red-count}; a memory-frugal streaming algorithm ---
Proposition~\ref{prop:red-comm} against a communication bound. \emph{Second, what does
the source's hardness actually say?} The target inherits the source's resolution
exactly: from Set Cover one may import a $\ln n$ inapproximability (under UGC) but not
NP-hardness of approximation beyond it; from Disjointness, an $\Omega(m)$ space bound
but nothing about time; from SETH, quantitative time bounds conditioned on the
hypothesis. Picking the source closest in \emph{shape} to the target is what keeps the
gadget small: covering structure suggests Set Cover, subgraph patterns suggest CLIQUE
or hypercliques, private estimation suggests InnerProduct, enumeration suggests
Hamiltonian path.

\begin{reachbox}{When to Reach for Reductions}
Reach whenever a claim of the form ``no system can do X'' or ``doing X requires
resource Y'' must be justified --- or, constructively, whenever a new problem should
inherit an existing algorithm. Then run the recipe:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Pick the canonical source problem closest in shape: Set Cover for covering and
  outlier budgets, CLIQUE/biclique for subgraph patterns, InnerProduct for private
  estimation, \#Set Cover or the permanent for counting, Disjointness for stream space,
  Hamiltonian path for exact combinatorial targets.
\item Build the gadget $f$ (instances forward) and the decoder $g$ (quality backward);
  fix the currency and the matching rung of the ladder.
\item State explicitly which hypothesis the reader inherits: a proof (NP-, \#P-), a
  conjecture (UGC), or a quantitative hypothesis (SETH, combinatorial BMM, a
  communication bound). Conditional claims must wear their conditions.
\item Audit the parameter blow-up of the gadget in \emph{every} regime, and the ratio
  algebra ($\alpha\beta r$, Proposition~\ref{prop:red-ratio}) --- a sloppy parameter
  map makes the bound vacuous somewhere.
\item Run the machinery backwards for the upper-bound side (reduce to LP feasibility,
  submodular maximization, dynamic programming); a dichotomy is two reductions glued
  at a common frontier.
\end{enumerate}
\end{reachbox}

\section{Gadget reductions: privacy under continual release}
\label{sec:red-continual}

The exemplar is a PODS'26 paper on differential privacy for streaming graphs
\citep{sigmod26-136}\pods. When sensitive data arrives as a stream --- friendship edges,
transactions, sensor readings --- a private system cannot wait for the stream to end:
it must keep answering queries while the data grows, and every published answer leaks
a little. The \emph{continual release} model asks how accurate such a stream of answers
can be while satisfying differential privacy throughout. Prior work had established
lower bounds only in restricted settings; this paper improves them to polynomial bounds
for three graph statistics systems actually maintain --- the maximum matching size, the
degree histogram, and the $k$-core number --- proving that any private incremental
mechanism must incur additive error polynomial in the stream length $T$ or the graph
size $n$. It also gives the first lower bounds for Top-$K$ norm estimation, extends the
technique to monotone symmetric norms, and matches the lower bounds with binary-tree
mechanisms whose error is polynomial with better-behaved constants --- so the frontier
it draws is essentially tight.

\paragraph{Problem.}
Work in the continual-release streaming model: an $n$-vertex graph evolves over $T$
timesteps through a stream of edge insertions (\emph{incremental} streams), and at every
timestep the mechanism must output an estimate of a graph statistic $g$
(\emph{maintaining} it). Privacy is event-level differential privacy: any two streams
differing in a single update event must yield indistinguishable output distributions.
The mechanism has \emph{additive error} $\alpha$ if, with probability at least $2/3$,
at every timestep its estimate is within $\alpha$ of the true value $g(G)$ of the
current graph.

\sidenote{The ``exact setting'' in the paper's statements means the mechanism maintains
the exact statistic up to this additive error --- no multiplicative slack is allowed.
The exponents $a, b$ are problem-specific: $1/4$ and $1/4$ for matching, $1/4$ and
$1/2$ for the degree histogram, $1/8$ and $1/4$ for $k$-core.}

\paragraph{Guarantee.}

\begin{theorem}[Continual-release lower bound, Theorem~1 of the paper]\label{thm:red-continual}
Let $\varepsilon \in [0,1]$ and $\delta \in [0,1/3]$. Any $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP
incremental mechanism in the exact setting for maintaining the size of the maximum
matching, the degree histogram, or the core number of a vertex must have additive error
\[
\alpha \;=\; \Omega\bigl(\min\{\, T^{a},\, n^{b} \,\}\bigr),
\]
for problem-specific constants $a, b \in [1/8, 1/2]$. \eop
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{How the reduction is applied.}
The source problem is \textsc{InnerProduct}: a secret dataset $x \in \{0,1\}^d$ and
queries $q^1, \dots, q^m \in \{0,1\}^d$, with the mechanism answering every
$\langle x, q^j \rangle$; a packing argument shows any $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP
mechanism for it has error $\widetilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{d})$. The gadget is an
\emph{extendable bitwise-AND gadget}: a family of graphs $H_{x}^{j}$ and $Q_{x}^{j}$,
built from $v(d)$ vertices and $t(d)$ edges, in which the statistic's value jumps by
exactly $\langle x, q^j\rangle$ between two queryable stream prefixes, and flipping one
bit of $x$ changes one stream event --- event-level neighboring streams, so privacy of
the graph mechanism implies privacy of the inner-product answers. The transfer is the
paper's workhorse lemma:

\begin{lemma}[Gadget transfer, Lemma~1 of the paper, notation cleaned]\label{lem:red-gadget}
Let $g$ be a graph problem that admits an extendable bitwise-AND gadget with $v(d)$
vertices and $t(d)$ edges per inner-product dimension. Any $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP
event-level mechanism that maintains $g$ in the exact setting, on $n$-node graphs over
$T$ timesteps, with additive error $\alpha$, satisfies
\[
\alpha \;=\; \widetilde{\Omega}\bigl(\min\{\, t^{-}(T),\, v^{-}(n) \,\}\bigr),
\qquad f^{-}(x) = \sup\{\, d \mid f(d) \le x \,\}. \eopm
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Query the mechanism just before and just after inserting the edges that encode $q^j$;
the two estimates differ by $\langle x, q^j \rangle$ up to $2\alpha$, so the graph
mechanism is an $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP mechanism for \textsc{InnerProduct} with
error $2\alpha$; the packing bound $\widetilde{\Omega}(\sqrt d)$ forces $\alpha \ge
\widetilde{\Omega}(\sqrt d)$ with $d$ set to the largest dimension the budget
$T, n$ admits. (Full proof in Section~\ref{sec:red-appendix}.)
\end{proof}

The $\delta$-tuning analogue is the \emph{parameter audit}: $d$ is chosen exactly as
$d = \min\{t^{-}(T), v^{-}(n)\}$, the largest dimension whose gadget fits both the
timestep budget $T$ and the vertex budget $n$. Lemma~2 of the paper supplies the
gadgets --- $v(d) = t(d) = O(d^2)$ for matching, $v(d) = O(d)$ and $t(d) = O(d^2)$ for
the degree histogram, $v(d) = O(d^2)$ and $t(d) = O(d^4)$ for $k$-core --- and the audit
turns them into exponents: matching gives $\alpha =
\widetilde{\Omega}(\min\{T^{1/4}, n^{1/4}\})$, the histogram $\alpha =
\widetilde{\Omega}(\min\{T^{1/4}, n^{1/2}\})$, and $k$-core $\alpha =
\widetilde{\Omega}(\min\{T^{1/8}, n^{1/4}\})$ --- the constants $a, b \in [1/8, 1/2]$
of Theorem~\ref{thm:red-continual}, with the polylogarithmic factors absorbed.

\paragraph{What it buys.}
The system designer learns that continually maintaining these statistics privately is
polynomially expensive, \emph{and where the escape hatches are}: the paper's companion
upper bounds (Theorem~2 of the paper) achieve error $O_{\varepsilon,\delta}(
\min\{T^{c}, n^{d}\})$ with $c, d \in [1/6, 1/2]$ via binary-tree mechanisms and DP
composition, while relaxing to the static setting --- or allowing a $(1+\zeta)$
multiplicative approximation --- admits mechanisms with polylogarithmic error. The
reduction did not just import hardness; it located the exact regime where hardness
lives.

\section{Gap reductions: clustering with set outliers}
\label{sec:red-outliers}

The exemplar is a SIGMOD'26 paper on robust clustering \citep{sigmod26-289}. Clustering
with outliers is a workhorse of robust analytics: alongside the $k$ centers, the system
may discard some data as unrepresentative. Prior models discard individual points; this
paper discards whole \emph{sets} from a candidate family --- hyper-rectangles covering
a spatial region (a maintenance window, a blackout area), or sets of tuples induced by
a relational join, so that clustering can run directly over the join without
materializing it. The paper develops near-linear-time $(2+\varepsilon, 2, O(1))$-
approximation algorithms, coresets that shrink the instance to
$O(\min\{n, k^2 z, km\})$ points, and high-probability algorithms for the relational
variants --- and justifies the shape of these guarantees with a hardness result: under
the Unique Games Conjecture, no polynomial-time algorithm can keep all three quality
dials at their optimum, and the outlier budget in particular cannot escape a
logarithmic blow-up.

\paragraph{Problem.}
In \emph{clustering with set outliers}, the input is a set $P$ of $n$ points, a family
$\mathcal{H}$ of $m$ subsets of $P$ whose union covers $P$, and integer budgets
$k, z > 0$. A solution picks at most $k$ centers and at most $z$ \emph{outlier sets}
from $\mathcal{H}$; all points covered by the chosen sets are discarded, and the goal
is to minimize the clustering cost (here, the $k$-center objective) of the remaining
points. An $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)$-\emph{approximation} inflates the cost by at most
$\alpha$ and the two budgets by at most $\beta$ and $\gamma$. The relational variant
takes $P$ to be the answer set of an acyclic join query accessed through counting and
sampling oracles.

\sidenote{The \emph{Unique Games Conjecture} (UGC) asserts that a certain two-prover
game is hard to distinguish with high probability; taken with it, Set Cover admits no
polynomial-time $(1-\varepsilon)\ln n$-approximation for any $\varepsilon > 0$. It is
the standard conditional source for covering-type inapproximability.}

\paragraph{Guarantee.}

\begin{theorem}[Inapproximability under UGC, Theorem of the paper]\label{thm:red-ugc}
If the Unique Games Conjecture is true, there exists no $(1, f - \varepsilon,
\gamma)$-approximation algorithm that runs in polynomial time for the problem, where
$f = o(\log n)$, $\gamma = o(|\mathcal{H}|)$ is any sublinear value, and
$\varepsilon$ any positive real number. \eop
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{How the reduction is applied.}
The source is Set Cover, on the ratio rung: the outlier-set budget is the coordinate
that carries the Set-Cover ratio, because inflating the budget by a factor $f$ is
exactly allowing $f \cdot z^{*}$ sets to cover the elements. The transfer is clean
enough to state in full:

\begin{lemma}[Set-Cover transfer, Lemma of the paper]\label{lem:red-cover}
If $\mathrm{Alg}$ is a polynomial-time $(1, f - \varepsilon, \gamma)$-approximation
algorithm for the set-outlier clustering problem, with $\gamma = o(|\mathcal{H}|)$
sublinear, then there exists a polynomial-time $(f - \varepsilon)$-approximation
algorithm for Set Cover. \eop
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The gadget is a line--point encoding: element $x_i$ becomes the point $p_i$ at
coordinate $i$ of the real line; each set $y$ becomes the outlier set $\{p_i : x_i \in
y\}$; padding points, each with its own singleton outlier set, complete the family.
With no centers, the cost is zero exactly when the chosen outlier sets cover every
element-point, so a clustering solution within the inflated budgets is a set cover of
the corresponding size. (Full proof in Section~\ref{sec:red-appendix}.)
\end{proof}

The UGC-conditional $(1-\varepsilon)\ln n$ threshold for Set Cover then crosses over:
no $f = o(\log n)$ outlier-budget factor, even with sublinear center-budget slack.
The same paper runs the machinery constructively --- the run-backwards half of the
recipe: an LP feasibility relaxation with greedy rounding and a weight-charging
argument delivers $(2 + \varepsilon, 2f, 2 + \varepsilon)$-approximations, coresets
shrink the instance, and the relational variant reuses the LP through join sampling
and counting oracles, with high-probability guarantees.

\paragraph{What it buys.}
The lower bound tells the system \emph{which budget must pay}: the paper's algorithms
inflate the outlier budget by a logarithmic factor, and Theorem~\ref{thm:red-ugc}
certifies that (conditioned on UGC) essentially nothing better is possible --- so the
practitioner can treat $\Theta(\log n)$ outlier slack as the price of admission rather
than an artifact of the analysis, and the join-based variants inherit the same
frontier.

\section{Both directions at once: volume-based answer diversity}
\label{sec:red-diversity}

The exemplar is a SIGMOD'26 paper on diversifying query answers \citep{sigmod26-350}.
When a query returns millions of tuples, a system shows the user a few
\emph{diverse} answers rather than the top ones; choosing them means maximizing a
diversity function over answer sets. The familiar diversity measures are
distance-based --- pairs of answers compared under Hamming or Euclidean distance ---
but these functions fail to be submodular, so the greedy heuristics systems actually
run come without guarantees, and the best provable ratios depend on the metric. The
paper proposes \emph{volume-based} diversity: each answer maps to a measurable set (its
``volume''), and the diversity of an answer set is the measure of its union --- a
direct generalization of the classic multi-attribute notion. The payoff is a single
theorem pair: diversity is always monotone and submodular, so one greedy routine
delivers the optimal $(1 - 1/e)$ approximation for \emph{every} polynomially computable
volume assignment --- and a matching Max-Coverage reduction shows no better ratio is
possible.

\paragraph{Problem.}
Fix a query $Q$ over a database $D$ with answer set $Q(D)$. A \emph{volume assignment}
attaches to every answer $s$ a measurable set $\beta(s)$ in a measure space
$(\mathcal{U}, \mu)$:

\begin{definition}[Volume-based diversity, Definition of the paper]\label{def:red-volume}
Given a volume assignment $\Omega = (\mathcal{U}, \mu, \beta)$, the diversity of a
finite answer set $S$ is
\[
\delta_\Omega(S) \;=\; \mu\Bigl(\, \bigcup_{s \in S} \beta(s) \Bigr). \eopm
\]
\emph{DiverseSet} asks for $S \subseteq Q(D)$ with $|S| = k$ maximizing
$\delta_\Omega(S)$; \emph{DiverseTopk} asks for ranked enumeration.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Guarantee.}

\begin{proposition}[Diversity is submodular, Proposition of the paper]\label{prop:red-submod}
Let $\Omega$ be any volume assignment over a universe $\mathcal{U}$ of possible
solutions. Then $\delta_\Omega$ is monotone and submodular: for
$S_1 \subseteq S_2$ and any answer $a$,
\[
\delta_\Omega(S_1 \cup \{a\}) - \delta_\Omega(S_1) \;\ge\;
\delta_\Omega(S_2 \cup \{a\}) - \delta_\Omega(S_2). \eopm
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}[One ratio for all volumes, results of the paper]\label{cor:red-greedy}
For every volume assignment $\Omega$ computable in polynomial time and every $k \ge 1$,
the greedy algorithm computes $S$ with $\delta_\Omega(S) \ge (1 - \tfrac{1}{e}) \cdot
\max_{|S'| = k} \delta_\Omega(S')$ in polynomial time. No polynomial-time algorithm
achieves ratio $(1 - \tfrac{1}{e} + \varepsilon)$ for all volume assignments unless
$\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$. \eop
\end{corollary}

\paragraph{How the reduction is applied.}
This exemplar runs the tool in \emph{both directions}. Backwards, it reduces
DiverseSet to monotone submodular maximization: Proposition~\ref{prop:red-submod}
certifies the structure, and the classical greedy analysis of Nemhauser, Wolsey, and
Fisher supplies the algorithm and the $(1 - 1/e)$ ratio --- one inherited theorem for
the whole family of measures. Forwards, it imports hardness from Max Coverage on the
ratio rung: the reduction encodes a coverage instance into a volume assignment over
the atom algebra of the sets, so that each tuple's volume is exactly what it covers
and $\delta_\Omega(S)$ is the covered mass --- and since Max Coverage cannot be
approximated beyond $(1 - 1/e)$ unless $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$, neither can
volume-based diversity. A second, purely combinatorial gadget handles the exact
variant: for the acyclic path query $Q(x_1,\dots,x_n) \,{:\!-}\, E(x_1,x_2), \dots,
E(x_{n-1},x_n)$, answers are walks in the input graph and the diversity of a single
answer counts the distinct vertices it visits, so a walk of diversity $n$ exists
exactly when the graph has a Hamiltonian path --- NP-hardness already for $k = 1$. The
workhorse is the measure-algebra proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:red-submod}: the
marginal gain of adding $a$ is $\mu(\beta(a) \setminus \bigcup_{s \in S}\beta(s))$,
which shrinks as $S$ grows (full proof in Section~\ref{sec:red-appendix}).

\sidenote{For acyclic CQs the greedy step itself reduces further: finding the answer
that maximizes marginal diversity is cast as a sum-product query over the tropical
semiring, solvable in $O(|Q| \cdot |D|)$ time --- so $k$ greedy steps cost
$O(k \cdot |Q| \cdot |D|)$, with ranked-enumeration preprocessing for DiverseTopk.}

\paragraph{What it buys.}
The system keeps \emph{one} diversification operator --- greedy marginal-diversity ---
with a single certified ratio $(1 - 1/e)$ for every volume measure a user may define,
instead of per-metric heuristics with per-metric guarantees; the matched lower bound
certifies the ratio is optimal, and the tropical-semiring evaluation makes each step
run in linear time over acyclic joins.

\section[Counting reductions: Shapley values]{Counting reductions: Shapley values for aggregate queries}
\label{sec:red-shapley}

The exemplar is a SIGMOD'26 paper on explaining query answers \citep{sigmod26-375}.
When an aggregate answer surprises a user --- why is this average so high? --- the
natural question is \emph{which input facts drove it}. The Shapley value, imported
from cooperative game theory, is the canonical fair answer: it averages a fact's
marginal contribution over all orders in which the endogenous facts could have
arrived. Prior work pinned the complexity of Shapley values for Boolean conjunctive
queries; this paper extends the analysis to \emph{aggregate} queries --- sum, max,
min, and quantiles, with value functions applied at head positions --- and draws the
full frontier: computation is polynomial for hierarchical queries and \#P-complete
just past the frontier, with \#P-completeness already for concrete small queries.
Along the way it derives closed-form Shapley formulas for averages and quantiles, and
uses the axioms of the Shapley value itself as an algorithmic tool on the tractable
side.

\paragraph{Problem.}
A database $D$ splits into an exogenous part $D_{\mathrm{ex}}$ (fixed) and an
endogenous part $D_{\mathrm{endo}}$ (the facts that may be added or removed). For an
aggregate query $A$ the attribution question is:

\begin{definition}[Shapley value of a fact, Definition of the paper]\label{def:red-shapley}
For $f \in D_{\mathrm{endo}}$, let $\sigma_{<f}$ be the endogenous facts preceding $f$
in a uniformly random permutation $\sigma$. The Shapley value of $f$ is
\[
\varphi(f, A) \;=\; \frac{1}{|D_{\mathrm{endo}}|!}
\sum_{\sigma \in \Sigma(D_{\mathrm{endo}})}
\Bigl[\, A(D_{\mathrm{ex}} \cup \sigma_{<f} \cup \{f\}) -
A(D_{\mathrm{ex}} \cup \sigma_{<f}) \,\Bigr], \eopm
\]
the expected amount by which inserting $f$ changes the aggregate.
\end{definition}

The aggregate queries are $A_\alpha = \alpha \circ Q$ for a self-join-free
conjunctive query $Q$ and $\alpha \in \{\mathrm{sum}, \mathrm{max}, \mathrm{min}\}$
or a fixed quantile, composed with a \emph{localized} value function applied to the
values at head positions of $Q$.

\sidenote{A query is \emph{hierarchical} if whenever two variables share an atom, the
variables of one are contained in the other's atom set --- equivalently, the
connected-variable sets form a laminar family. It is the classical frontier between
easy and hard conjunctive-query evaluation, and this paper shows the same line governs
explanation.}

\paragraph{Guarantee.}

\begin{theorem}[Tractability frontier, Theorems of the paper, notation cleaned]
\label{thm:red-shapley}
Let $Q$ be a CQ without self-joins, $A_\alpha = \alpha \circ Q$ with $\alpha \in
\{\mathrm{sum}, \mathrm{max}, \mathrm{min}\}$ or a fixed quantile. If $Q$ is
hierarchical, then $\varphi(f, A_\alpha)$ is computable in polynomial time, given $D$
and a fact $f$, for every localized value function. Otherwise, there is a localized
value function such that computing $\varphi(f, A_\alpha)$, given $D$ and a fact $f$,
is \#P-complete. \eop
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{How the reduction is applied.}
The currency is a \emph{count}, so the rung is Definition~\ref{def:red-count} --- the
number of set covers, a \#P-complete quantity, must be recoverable from Shapley
values. The source is \#Set-Cover:

\begin{lemma}[\#Set-Cover transfer, Lemma of the paper, notation cleaned]\label{lem:red-shapley}
There is a polynomial-time algorithm that, given a \#Set-Cover instance $(X,
\mathcal{Y})$ with $|X| = n$, $|\mathcal{Y}| = m$, constructs databases $D_{q,r}$,
$q \in [n]$, $r \in [m]$, each with a distinguished fact $f$, such that the numbers
\[
Z_{i,j} \;=\; \#\bigl\{\, \mathcal{Z} \subseteq \mathcal{Y} \;:\;
|\mathcal{Z}| = j,\; \textstyle\bigcup_{Z \in \mathcal{Z}} Z \text{ covers exactly } i
\text{ elements of } X \,\bigr\}
\]
--- in particular the number of set covers, $\sum_j Z_{n,j}$ --- are computable in
polynomial time from the Shapley values $\varphi(f, A)$ over the $D_{q,r}$. Hence
computing Shapley values is \#P-hard. \eop
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Each database is built so that a coalition's aggregate encodes how much of $X$ the
corresponding subfamily covers: chosen sets become endogenous facts, sentinel facts
weight each covered element, and the fact $f$ flips the answer exactly when the
subfamily covers everything --- so $\varphi(f, A)$ is a known linear combination of
the $Z_{i,j}$, and the family $D_{q,r}$ provides enough independent combinations to
solve for all of them. Theorem~\ref{thm:red-shapley}'s hardness side follows, together
with a sibling reduction from the permanent (counting exact covers) for average-type
aggregates.
\end{proof}

On the tractable side the reduction runs backwards, through the axioms of the Shapley
value itself: linearity decomposes an aggregate into indicator queries, symmetry
equalizes the values of interchangeable facts, and null players --- facts that never
flip the answer --- contribute zero; for hierarchical $Q$ these identities collapse
the sum of Definition~\ref{def:red-shapley} into a polynomial-time dynamic program
over the query's variable tree.

\paragraph{What it buys.}
An explanation engine learns exactly when fact-ranking is cheap: within the
hierarchical fragment, responsibility scores come at evaluation cost, and one step
outside it they are \#P-complete even for two-atom queries --- so a system should
either restrict explanations to the fragment or budget for approximation, and the
classical query-tractability map doubles as the explanation-tractability map.

\section{Advanced usage and further reading}
\label{sec:red-advanced}

Three refinements round out the toolbox: quantitative hypotheses when the target claim
is a refined running time, chained reductions when one hop is not enough, and transfer
lemmmas when the reduction's purpose is to build an algorithm. This section states the
pattern, sketches two harder exemplars, and points to the literature.

\subsection{Fine-grained and conditional reductions}
\label{sec:red-finegrained}

When the claim is not ``no polynomial algorithm'' but ``no
$O(N^{2-\varepsilon})$ / quasilinear / constant-delay algorithm'', the source must be
quantitative: SETH, the hyperclique hypothesis, combinatorial Boolean matrix
multiplication, or listing lower bounds. The machinery is
Proposition~\ref{prop:red-fine} with the parameter audit as the proof of correctness.
The corpus uses it for query-evaluation dichotomies: direct access to conjunctive
query answers in aggregate-driven orders admits quasilinear preprocessing exactly when
the query has no chordless path between order variables --- the hard side encodes
hyperclique detection under the Hyperclique hypothesis and SETH
\citep{sigmod26-109}\pods. A second shape runs the reduction against a
\emph{randomized} solver: for uniform sampling from join--project queries, a sampler
restarted $O(\mathsf{OUT}\log\mathsf{OUT})$ times would list all answers --- the
coupon-collector argument turned backwards --- so the assumed listing lower bound
rules out fast samplers, and combinatorial BMM instantiates the hypothesis for matrix
queries \citep{sigmod26-243}\pods.

\subsection{Two advanced exemplars, sketched}
\label{sec:red-sketches}

Both exemplars of this section push past a single many-one hop; each is sketched in
one paragraph, and the reduction chain of the first is transcribed in the
Appendix (Section~\ref{sec:red-appendix}).

\paragraph{Reduction chains: pseudodeterministic streaming \citep{sigmod26-357}.}
A streaming algorithm is \emph{pseudodeterministic} if on every input it outputs a
\emph{canonical} value with probability at least $2/3$ --- the reproducibility a
debugger or an audit needs --- and \emph{representation oblivious} if its output
distribution depends only on the multiset of stream elements, not their order. The
paper proves that any one-pass representation-oblivious pseudodeterministic (ROPD)
algorithm $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-approximating $F_0$ (the number of distinct elements)
requires $\Omega(n)$ space --- against the $O(\operatorname{polylog} n)$ classics ---
and $\Omega(n/t(n))$ for $t(n)$ passes, matching a deterministic algorithm. The
argument is a \emph{chain} on the communication rung (Proposition~\ref{prop:red-comm}):
an ROPD $F_0$ estimator becomes an ROPD protocol for Promise-Union (do Alice's and
Bob's sets union to more than $3n/4$ of the universe?), which becomes a
pseudodeterministic protocol for Disjointness, contradicting the classical
$\Omega(m)$ communication bound; obliviousness is what carries equivalence classes of
streams through each hop, and pseudodeterminism is what makes the final contradiction
deterministic. The constructive side checks that the classical sketches (BJKST, AMS,
linear sketches) \emph{are} representation oblivious --- the restriction bites exactly
where canonical outputs are impossible.

\paragraph{Transfer lemmas: one algorithm for many query problems \citep{sigmod26-264}.}
Counting satisfying subsets, evaluating tuple-independent probabilistic databases, and
maximizing a repaired query's multiplicity under bag-set semantics look like three
problems, but over hierarchical queries they are one: the paper annotates facts in a
\emph{two-monoid} algebra of decomposable provenance trees and runs a single
variable-elimination algorithm (eliminate private variables, merge duplicate atoms).
Correctness is itself a reduction statement --- a transfer lemma: any map $\varphi$
from provenance trees to another two-monoid that commutes with the two operations on
decomposable trees carries the algorithm's output to the correct value for the target
problem, so each application (probability, counting, repair) is a homomorphism
instantiation rather than a new algorithm, and the whole runs with a linear number of
monoid operations. The frontier is closed with a many-one reduction in the other
direction: for every non-hierarchical query, the repair problem is NP-complete via a
biclique gadget --- a full dichotomy in the reachbox's sense.

\item Chandra and Merlin \citep{reduction-bg6} --- conjunctive queries and

  (Example~\ref{ex:red-cq}).
\end{itemize}

\begin{recap}
\item \textbf{What the tool is.} A reduction is a two-way conduit: a polynomial gadget
  carries instances of a hard source problem into the target, and a decoder carries
  quality backward --- so any solver for the target solves the source, importing the
  source's hardness at exactly its resolution.
\item \textbf{Which rung when.} Ruling out algorithms: many-one (Definition~\ref{def:red-manyone}).
  Ruling out approximations: $L$-/gap reductions against a covering source
  (Definition~\ref{def:red-lred}). Ruling out counters: \#P Turing reductions
  (Definition~\ref{def:red-count}). Refined time bounds: fine-grained transfer under
  SETH/BMM/hyperclique hypotheses (Proposition~\ref{prop:red-fine}). Stream space:
  one-pass-to-communication conversion (Proposition~\ref{prop:red-comm}).
\item \textbf{Why the audit matters.} The gadget's parameter blow-up must be checked in
  every regime --- the privacy gadget of Section~\ref{sec:red-continual} chose its
  dimension $d = \min\{t^{-}(T), v^{-}(n)\}$ exactly so that both budgets bind, turning
  gadget sizes directly into the exponents of Theorem~\ref{thm:red-continual}.
\item \textbf{What the corpus showed.} Continual-release privacy
  (Section~\ref{sec:red-continual}): InnerProduct gadgets import error bounds into DP
  stream maintenance. Set-outlier clustering (Section~\ref{sec:red-outliers}): a
  line--point encoding transfers Set Cover's UGC threshold to the outlier budget.
  Answer diversity (Section~\ref{sec:red-diversity}): one problem reduced to submodular
  maximization (algorithm) and from Max Coverage (hardness) --- both directions at
  once. Shapley attribution (Section~\ref{sec:red-shapley}): \#Set-Cover counting
  reductions draw a dichotomy at the hierarchical frontier.
\item \textbf{Advanced usage.} Fine-grained dichotomies under SETH/hyperclique/BMM
  hypotheses and coupon-collector reversals against randomized solvers
  (Section~\ref{sec:red-finegrained}); reduction chains for pseudodeterministic
  streaming and algebraic transfer lemmas that unify algorithms
  (Section~\ref{sec:red-sketches}). Deferred proofs live in the
  Appendix (Section~\ref{sec:red-appendix}).
\end{recap}

\label{sec:red-appendix}

\subsubsection*{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:red-gadget} (gadget transfer,
Section~\ref{sec:red-continual})}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} Any $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP event-level mechanism
maintaining exactly a graph problem $g$ that admits an extendable bitwise-AND gadget
with $v(d)$ vertices and $t(d)$ edges, on $n$-node graphs over $T$ timesteps, has
additive error $\alpha = \widetilde{\Omega}(\min\{t^{-}(T), v^{-}(n)\})$, where
$f^{-}(x) = \sup\{d \mid f(d) \le x\}$.\eop

\begin{proof}
Let $\mathcal{A}$ be such a mechanism with additive error $\alpha$, and let
$(x, q^1, \dots, q^m)$ be an \textsc{InnerProduct} instance of dimension $d$, with
$d$ chosen as $d = \min\{t^{-}(T), v^{-}(n)\}$, so the gadget's $v(d)$ vertices and
$t(d)$ edges fit the budgets $n$ and $T$. Emulate an $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP
mechanism for \textsc{InnerProduct}: initialize the stream with the gadget's
$H_{\mathrm{init}}$; insert the edge $e_i$ iff the data bit $x_i = 1$, giving the
graph $H_x^1$; then, for each query $q^j$, (i) query $\mathcal{A}$, obtaining an
estimate $\hat e^j$ of $g(H_x^j)$; (ii) insert the query edges $S^j$ (those whose
index corresponds to a query bit set to one), giving $Q_x^j$, query $\mathcal{A}$
again, obtaining $\hat q^j$; (iii) output $\hat q^j - \hat e^j$ as the estimate of
$\langle x, q^j \rangle$; (iv) insert the neutral edges $T^j$, giving $H_x^{j+1}$,
ready for the next query. By the gadget's guarantee, the true values satisfy
$\mathrm{dec}_j(g(Q_x^j)) - \mathrm{dec}_j(g(H_x^j)) = \langle x, q^j \rangle$, and
the accuracy guarantee gives $|\hat q^j - g(Q_x^j)| \le \alpha$ and
$|\hat e^j - g(H_x^j)| \le \alpha$ simultaneously with probability $2/3$, so every
inner product is answered within $2\alpha$. Changing one bit of $x$ changes one event
of the stream (edge $e_i$ inserted or not), so event-level DP of $\mathcal{A}$ makes
the emulation $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP --- contradicting the packing lower bound
$\widetilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{d})$ for privately answering \textsc{InnerProduct} unless
$\alpha \ge \widetilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{d}) = \widetilde{\Omega}(\min\{t^{-}(T),
v^{-}(n)\}^{1/2})$.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:red-cover} (Set-Cover transfer,
Section~\ref{sec:red-outliers})}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} A polynomial-time $(1, f-\varepsilon,
\gamma)$-approximation for set-outlier clustering with sublinear $\gamma$ yields a
polynomial-time $(f-\varepsilon)$-approximation for Set Cover.\eop

\begin{proof}
Given a Set Cover instance $(X, \mathcal{Y})$, $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_{n'}\}$, build the
clustering instance: for each element $x_i$, a point $p_i$ at coordinate $i$ of the
real line; $\ell$ additional padding points $q_1, \dots, q_\ell$ at coordinates
$2n' + 1, \dots, 2n' + \ell$; for each set $y \in \mathcal{Y}$, the outlier set
$H_y = \{p_i : x_i \in y\}$; and singleton outlier sets $\{q_j\}$ for the padding
points. Set the center budget to zero, so the objective is to cover all points by
outlier sets, and the cost of any uncovered point exceeds any cover-based optimum
(with the padding singletons forcing $\ell$ of the budget to be spent
canonically, $\ell$ chosen polynomially bounded).
\emph{Yes to yes:} if $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{Y}$ covers $X$ with
$|\mathcal{C}| \le z$, then choosing the outlier sets $\{H_y\}_{y \in \mathcal{C}}$
plus the $\ell$ padding singletons covers every point within budget $z + \ell$, at
cost zero. \emph{Solution to cover:} conversely, any solution of cost zero (the cost
factor $1$ forces this) covers every element-point using some outlier sets; discarding
the padding singletons, the corresponding sets $y$ form a cover of $X$ whose size is
within the outlier-budget factor of the optimum cover size. Running the assumed
$(1, f - \varepsilon, \gamma)$-approximation on this instance (the sublinear center
inflation $\gamma$ is spent only on the padding construction) therefore returns a
cover of size at most $(f - \varepsilon)$ times the optimum --- a polynomial-time
$(f - \varepsilon)$-approximation to Set Cover, which the UGC-conditional
$(1-\varepsilon)\ln n$ threshold rules out for $f = o(\log n)$.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:red-submod} (submodularity,
Section~\ref{sec:red-diversity})}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} For every volume assignment $\Omega = (\mathcal{U},
\mu, \beta)$, the diversity $\delta_\Omega(S) = \mu(\bigcup_{s \in S} \beta(s))$ is
monotone and submodular.\eop

\begin{proof}
\emph{Monotonicity:} $S_1 \subseteq S_2$ implies $\bigcup_{s \in S_1} \beta(s)
\subseteq \bigcup_{s \in S_2} \beta(s)$, and $\mu$ is monotone. \emph{Submodularity:}
let $S_1 \subseteq S_2$ and $a \notin S_2$ (if $a \in S_2$ both sides are zero). For
any $S$,
\[
\delta_\Omega(S \cup \{a\}) - \delta_\Omega(S)
\;=\; \mu\Bigl(\beta(a) \setminus \bigcup_{s \in S} \beta(s)\Bigr),
\]
by disjoint decomposition of the measure ($\mu(B \cup C) = \mu(B) + \mu(C \setminus
B)$). Since $\bigcup_{s \in S_1} \beta(s) \subseteq \bigcup_{s \in S_2} \beta(s)$, we
have $\beta(a) \setminus \bigcup_{s \in S_2}\beta(s) \subseteq \beta(a) \setminus
\bigcup_{s \in S_1} \beta(s)$, and monotonicity of $\mu$ gives
$\mu(\beta(a) \setminus \bigcup_{s \in S_2} \beta(s)) \le \mu(\beta(a) \setminus
\bigcup_{s \in S_1} \beta(s))$, which is the stated inequality. (This is the paper's
proof; the Corollary~\ref{cor:red-greedy} guarantee then follows from the classical
greedy analysis of monotone submodular maximization.)
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{From estimator to protocol (Section~\ref{sec:red-sketches})}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} If a one-pass ROPD streaming algorithm
$(\varepsilon, \delta)$-approximates $F_0$ over universe $[n]$ using $s$ bits, then
there is a pseudodeterministic protocol of communication $O(s)$ for a
Promise-Union$_n$ problem whose communication complexity is $\Omega(n)$; hence
$s = \Omega(n)$ (Theorem 3.1 of the paper), and $t$-pass algorithms similarly need
$\Omega(n/t)$ space.\eop

\begin{proof}
\emph{Estimator to protocol.} Alice holds $A \subseteq [n]$, Bob holds $B \subseteq
[n]$. Alice converts $A$ to its canonical stream $D(A)$ (the elements in
lexicographic order), runs the estimator on it, and sends Bob the final memory state
--- $s$ bits, exactly Proposition~\ref{prop:red-comm}. Bob continues the execution
with $D(B)$, so the algorithm processes the stream of $A \cup B$, and outputs $1$ iff
the returned estimate is at least $n/2$. On the promise (unions of size $\ge 3n/4$
versus $\le n/4$), any $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-approximation of $F_0 = |A \cup B|$
with $\varepsilon, \delta \le 1/3$ decides correctly with probability $\ge 2/3$.
\emph{Obliviousness carries the class:} $D(A) \cdot D(B)$ is a canonical
representative of the $R_0$-equivalence class of streams over $A \cup B$, so
representation obliviousness makes the output distribution depend only on the union;
pseudodeterminism supplies a canonical value $g$, and Bob's output equals the fixed
predicate $h(A,B) = \mathbf{1}[g(D(A)\cdot D(B)) \ge n/2]$ with probability $2/3$ ---
the protocol itself is pseudodeterministic. \emph{The final hop:} disjointness
embeds into the promise gap by padding both players' inputs so that disjoint inputs
land above the high threshold and intersecting ones below it, yielding a
pseudodeterministic protocol for Disj$_m$ with communication $o(m)$ --- contradicting
the classical $\Omega(m)$ bound. The multi-pass bound ships the state back and
forth once per pass: communication $O(s \cdot t)$, so $s = \Omega(n/t)$.
\end{proof}
