\label{ch:ind}

\begin{objectives}
\item Recognize the three substrates: execution steps, recursive structure, measures.
\item State an invariant, close base and step cases, audit every operation.
\item Strengthen an invariant that will not close; pair it with a measure for costs.
\item Dissect a paper's proof: induction variable, invariant, step case, payoff.
\end{objectives}

Induction turns a claim about an unbounded process --- millions of cache fills, node
eliminations, rewrite steps, recursive calls --- into two finite checks: the claim holds
at the start, and one more step cannot break it. For a systems paper this is the
instrument of \emph{component} correctness. No grand named theorem is involved; the
corpus pattern is thousands of small, unnamed invariants --- half-page lemmas asserting
that one more elimination step, recursive call, or rewrite keeps a data structure
consistent --- with each cache, rule, or decomposition step verified locally and the
global guarantee assembled from the local ones. Induction is also the standard
scaffolding for complexity proofs: the same step-by-step argument that certifies what a
component computes carries the per-step cost accounting that becomes a running-time
bound.

In the SIGMOD'26 corpus, 36 tagged papers lean on induction --- the third most-used
tool, behind reductions and concentration inequalities --- spanning graph analytics,
join processing, entity resolution, and query optimization. The pairing
\emph{correctness}~$\times$~\emph{induction}, with 29 papers, is the single most
frequent property--tool co-occurrence of the survey, and induction also powers 21
papers tagged \emph{upper-bound}: the invariant proves the algorithm right, the
accompanying decreasing measure proves it fast. This chapter develops the three
substrates and the craft first; Section~\ref{sec:ind-advanced} adds strengthened and
nested invariants and two harder exemplars.

\section{The tool: induction and invariants}
\label{sec:ind-tool}

The problem class is uniform across applications. A component evolves by discrete local
steps --- an algorithm executes operations, an elimination pipeline removes one node, a
rewrite system fires one rule, a recursive evaluator makes one call --- and the claim we
need is global: \emph{every} answer produced is correct, \emph{every} intermediate
structure stays within its budget, \emph{no} reachable configuration drifts. The number
of configurations is astronomically larger than the code, so the claim cannot be
checked state by state. What induction delivers is a predicate $I$ on single
configurations --- the \emph{invariant} --- with two provable properties: it holds
initially, and each single step preserves it. Those two finite checks certify the
unbounded behavior. Every inductive argument in the corpus is an instance of this
shape, differing only in \emph{what the steps are}: states of a run, pieces of a
recursively built object, or values of a decreasing counter.

\begin{remarkbox}[Notation]
$I$ always denotes the predicate being propagated (the invariant); $\Gamma$ the set of
operations; $m\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{N}$ a measure. \emph{Leaf-peeling} is the
default move on tree-shaped objects: remove a leaf, invoke the hypothesis on the
smaller object, extend by one step.
\end{remarkbox}

\subsection{The three substrates}
\label{sec:ind-ladder}

\emph{Substrate 1: execution steps.} The component is imperative: it induces a sequence
of states $s_0, s_1, \dots, s_t$, one per operation. This is the inductive-assertion
method of Floyd \citep{induction-bg1} and Hoare \citep{induction-bg2}, the
loop-invariant discipline of algorithms texts \citep{induction-bg4}, and Manna--Pnueli
\emph{safety} reasoning --- nothing bad ever becomes true \citep{induction-bg3}.

\begin{theorem}[Invariant preservation]\label{thm:ind-invariant}
Let $s_0$ be an initial state and $\Gamma$ a set of operations on states. Suppose the
predicate $I$ satisfies (i) $I(s_0)$, and (ii) $I(s) \Rightarrow I(\operatorname{op}(s))$
for every $\operatorname{op} \in \Gamma$. Then $I(s)$ holds for every state $s$
reachable from $s_0$ by a finite sequence of operations; consequently, any
specification $P$ implied by $I$ ($I \Rightarrow P$) holds at every reachable state. \eop
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Induction on the length $t$ of the run $s_0 \to s_1 \to \dots \to s_t$. The base case
$t=0$ is (i). For the step, $s_t = \operatorname{op}(s_{t-1})$ for some operation, and
(ii) propagates $I(s_{t-1})$ to $I(s_t)$.
\end{proof}

\emph{Substrate 2: structural recursion.} The object is built recursively --- a tree
decomposition, an elimination forest, a recursive pseudo-tree, a relational expression
--- and the property is proved on the whole from the property on the parts. The
database workhorse is leaf-peeling: remove a leaf bag or leaf vertex, apply the
hypothesis to the smaller object, then extend by one step.

\begin{proposition}[Structural induction]\label{prop:ind-structural}
Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a class of objects generated from atoms by formation rules. If a
property $P$ holds for every atom, and each formation rule maps ingredients satisfying
$P$ to a result satisfying $P$, then every object of $\mathcal{C}$ satisfies $P$. In
particular, on trees it suffices to prove $P$ for leaves and to prove that a node whose
children's subtrees satisfy $P$ satisfies $P$ itself.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Induction on the number of formation steps used to build the object. Atoms use zero
steps. For a composite object, each immediate ingredient is built with fewer steps, so
the hypothesis gives $P$ on the ingredients, and the preservation assumption lifts $P$
to the composite. The leaf-peeling form is the same argument read from the root
downwards.
\end{proof}

\emph{Substrate 3: a decreasing measure.} Many arguments are phrased not over runs or
recursions but over a quantity that strictly decreases --- nodes left to eliminate, bags
left to peel, rewrite steps remaining. Structural induction is routinely converted into
this form by choosing a measure \citep{induction-bg5}, and the classical
least-counterexample principle closes both:

\begin{lemma}[Strong induction]\label{lem:ind-strong}
Let $m\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{N}$ be a measure. Suppose that for every object $X$,
if $P(X')$ holds for all $X'$ with $m(X') < m(X)$, then $P(X)$. Then $P(X)$ holds for
every object $X$. \eop
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If not, the set of counterexamples has an $m$-minimal element $X^{*}$ (the natural
numbers are well-ordered). Every $X'$ with $m(X') < m(X^{*})$ satisfies $P$, so the
hypothesis yields $P(X^{*})$ --- a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\sidenote{The measure earns a second salary: since each step decreases $m$ by at least
one, the component terminates in at most $m(s_0)$ steps, and attaching a per-step cost
to the induction turns the correctness proof into a running-time proof. This is how the
same argument underwrites both the \emph{correctness} pairing (29 papers) and the
\emph{upper-bound} pairing (21 papers) of this chapter's corpus.}

\subsection{How to choose a substrate}
\label{sec:ind-choose}

The selection is mechanical once one question is answered: \emph{what does one step
look like?} If the component is an explicit state machine --- a sweep with a stack, an
incrementally maintained index --- take execution steps and a state invariant
(Substrate 1). If the object of study is recursively defined and the claim is about the
finished object --- decompositions, pseudo-trees, expressions --- take structural
induction, leaf-peeling by default (Substrate 2). If the argument is really about a
process with no convenient state --- an elimination order, a rewrite sequence, a
peeling procedure --- phrase it as strong induction on the natural measure
(Substrate 3). All three are the same lemma in different clothes; pick the clothing
that makes the step case a purely local check.

\begin{reachbox}{When to Reach for Induction}
Reach whenever a component has state evolving by local steps and the claim is
``nothing drifts'' --- caches, elimination pipelines, rewrite rules, recursive
evaluators --- or whenever a guarantee must hold for \emph{all} inputs of unbounded
size. Then run the recipe:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Name the substrate and fix the induction variable precisely: the step index $i$
  of a run, the subtree rooted at a node $A$, the number of bags/nodes/rules left.
\item Write the invariant $I$ as a property of \emph{one} state or \emph{one} subtree,
  strong enough to imply the specification at final states (or at the whole object) and
  concrete enough to check in one step. Audit the step case over \emph{every} operation
  --- and the trivial base cases (empty input, single bag, zero eliminations): one
  missed case silently breaks the entire argument.
\item If the step case will not close, \emph{strengthen} $I$: carry more information
  (the exact block shape, the exact cache contents, both directions of an
  equivalence). Strengthening is the normal iteration of the method, not a failure ---
  see Proposition~\ref{prop:ind-strengthen}.
\item Pair $I$ with a strictly decreasing measure to obtain termination and per-step
  cost accounting --- the same induction then yields the time/space bound, not just
  correctness.
\end{enumerate}
\end{reachbox}

\section{Resistance-distance labels on small-treewidth graphs}
\label{sec:ind-resistance}

The exemplar is a SIGMOD'26 paper on exact graph analytics over small-treewidth graphs
\citep{sigmod26-307}. Resistance distance --- the effective resistance between two
nodes when the graph is viewed as an electrical network --- is a workhorse similarity
measure in graph databases: link prediction, recommendation, and graph-feature
extraction all use it because it averages over \emph{all} paths between two nodes, not
just the shortest one. Computing it exactly requires the pseudo-inverse of the graph
Laplacian, a matrix inversion that does not scale to large graphs, which is why
practitioners settle for approximations. The paper observes that on graphs of small
treewidth --- road networks, hierarchies, many social and biological networks ---
Gaussian elimination ordered along a tree decomposition factors the computation
\emph{once}, so that afterwards every pair query is answered from two small labels
alone. Its headline results: an index built in $O(n \cdot d_{\max} \cdot h_G^{2})$
time, labels of total size $O(n \cdot h_G)$, and \emph{exact} single-pair queries in
$O(h_G)$, where $h_G$ is the height of the tree decomposition and $d_{\max}$ the
maximum degree. The correctness of the elimination pipeline is where induction enters.

\paragraph{Problem.}
Let $G = (V,E)$ be a graph with $n$ nodes, Laplacian $L = D - A$, and
$r(s,t) = (\mathbf{e}_s - \mathbf{e}_t)^{\!\top} L^{+} (\mathbf{e}_s - \mathbf{e}_t)$
the resistance distance, where $L^{+}$ is the Moore--Penrose pseudo-inverse. The input
includes a tree decomposition of $G$ of height $h_G$ (the maximum number of bags on a
root-to-leaf path). The task is to preprocess $G$ into one label $\ell(v)$ per node so
that any query $r(s,t)$ is answered exactly from $\ell(s)$ and $\ell(t)$ alone, with
preprocessing, label size, and query time parameterized by $n$, $d_{\max}$, and $h_G$.

\paragraph{Guarantee.}

\begin{theorem}[Labelling scheme, complexity lemmas of the paper]\label{thm:ind-resistance}
Given $G$ and a tree decomposition of height $h_G$, the index construction computes
labels of total size $O(n \cdot h_G)$ in time $O(n \cdot d_{\max} \cdot h_G^{2})$, and
any single-pair query $r(s,t)$ is answered \emph{exactly} in $O(h_G)$ time from the two
labels; a single-source query over all $t$ costs $O(n \cdot h_G)$. \eop
\end{theorem}

\sidenote{Why exactness is plausible here: on a tree decomposition, eliminating a node
interacts only with its bag, so the inverse Laplacian factors into per-path pieces ---
the random-walk interpretation (resistance is a commute time) then reconstructs
$r(s,t)$ from the pieces on the two root paths, via cut properties: a vertex cut of
$s,t$ zeroes the cross terms.}

\paragraph{How induction is applied.}
The algorithm eliminates the nodes one at a time, in an order given by a traversal of
the tree decomposition, maintaining a shrinking \emph{state matrix} $S$ initialized to
the inverse of the Laplacian restricted to the current node set; eliminating node $v$
applies the rank-one update $S \mathrel{-}= S[:,v]\,S[:,v]^{\!\top} / S[v,v]$. The
induction variable is the number of eliminated nodes, and the induction hypothesis is
stated as an explicit invariant of the state:

\begin{lemma}[Elimination-state invariant, Lemma of the paper, notation cleaned]
\label{lem:ind-elim-state}
Let $U_1 \subseteq U_2 \subseteq V$. After the elimination of the nodes of
$U_2 \setminus U_1$, the state matrix satisfies
\[
S_{|U_2 \setminus U_1|}
\;=\;
\begin{pmatrix} (L_{|U_1})^{-1} & 0 \\[2pt] 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},
\]
i.e.\ all rows and columns of eliminated nodes are zero, and the block on the surviving
nodes is the inverse of the Laplacian restricted to $U_1$. \eop
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Induction on the number $k$ of eliminated nodes. Base case $k=0$: the state is
$(L_{|U_2})^{-1}$ by initialization. Step: partition the state around the next
eliminated node $v$; the rank-one update zeroes row and column $v$ and replaces the
surviving block by a Schur complement, which the block-inverse formula identifies with
the inverse of the Laplacian restricted to the remaining nodes. (Full proof in
Section~\ref{sec:ind-appendix}.)
\end{proof}

The labels store the elimination history: for each node, the columns of the state at
the moments its neighbors were eliminated (a subtree-contiguity property of the
traversal keeps each column segment addressable), and the query walks the two root
paths recombining those pieces through the resistance formula. Note the craft: the
invariant is an \emph{exact block shape}, not ``approximately correct'' --- strong
enough to pin every entry of the state, which is what makes the reconstruction exact.
The running-time bound rides the same induction: each of the $n$ elimination steps
touches at most $d_{\max}$ already-processed neighbors on segments of length $O(h_G)$,
giving the $O(n \cdot d_{\max} \cdot h_G^{2})$ total.

\paragraph{What it buys.}
The engine answers exact resistance-distance queries in time proportional to the
decomposition height rather than the graph size, after near-linear preprocessing on
low-treewidth data --- no approximation error, no sampling. The pattern --- eliminate
along a decomposition, store the elimination history as labels, reconstruct from labels
by an invariant --- transfers to any computation that factors through Gaussian
elimination on small-treewidth structure.

\section{Space--time plans for sum--product queries}
\label{sec:ind-sumprod}

The exemplar is a SIGMOD'26 paper on the space--time complexity of conjunctive-query
evaluation \citep{sigmod26-371}. A \emph{sum--product query} is a conjunctive query
evaluated over a semiring: ordinary joins (Boolean semiring), output counts (natural
numbers), tuple probabilities, or optima (tropical semiring). Worst-case optimal join
algorithms evaluate such queries in optimal time but materialize as they go, paying
full space for intermediate results; systems instead want caching plans that trade
time for space. The paper asks which points of the space--time plane a query can
occupy: it defines a ladder of plan classes --- pseudo-trees, pseudo-trees with
caching, tree-decomposition plans, and recursive pseudo-trees (RPTs) --- ordered by a
domination preorder on their $(s,t)$ exponent pairs, and identifies what each class
achieves. Headline results: every tree-decomposition plan is dominated by an RPT plan
(proved by the second induction exhibited below), the dominations are mostly strict,
witnessed by explicit path queries, and a Triple-$k$-Clique conjecture yields
conditional space--time lower bounds over the tropical semiring.

\paragraph{Problem.}
Fix a semiring $\mathbb{K}$ and a sum--product query
$Q(\mathbf{X}) = \prod_{i} R_i(\mathbf{X}_i)$; the database annotates tuples with
values of $\mathbb{K}$, an assignment multiplies its annotations, and $Q$ sums over
satisfying assignments. A \emph{pseudo-tree} is a directed tree over the variables of
$Q$ in which every atom's variables sit on a single root-to-leaf branch; a plan is a
pseudo-tree, possibly with caches at some vertices and applied recursively to
subqueries, and its exponents $s$ and $t$ (defined recursively in the paper) guarantee
evaluation in space $O(|D|^{s})$ and time $O(|D|^{t})$. Plan $P_1$ \emph{dominates}
$P_2$ if $t(P_1) \le t(P_2)$ and $s(P_1) \le s(P_2)$; a class dominates another if for
every query and every plan of the latter there is a dominating plan of the former.

\paragraph{Guarantee.}

\begin{theorem}[RPT evaluation, Theorem of the paper, compressed]\label{thm:ind-rpt}
Every recursive pseudo-tree plan $(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},\rho)$ of $Q$ with exponents
$s = s(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},\rho)$ and $t = t(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},\rho)$
correctly evaluates $Q$ over any semiring, in space $O(|D|^{s})$ and time
$O(|D|^{t})$. \eop
\end{theorem}

\sidenote{The benchmark being traded against: a worst-case optimal join evaluates a
full query in time $O(|D|^{\rho^{*}})$ with $\rho^{*}$ the fractional edge cover --- but
materializes intermediates, i.e.\ space exponent $\rho^{*}$ as well. The paper's
sublinear-space plans give up time exponents to buy space back.}

\paragraph{How induction is applied.}
Two inductions run side by side. The \emph{correctness} half is execution-step
induction over the run of the recursive algorithm: the induction variable is the
sequence of function calls $\mathrm{solve}(A, \mathbf{x})$, and the invariant says each
call returns exactly the value of the subquery rooted at $A$, with the cache holding
exactly that value:

\begin{lemma}[Cache invariant, Lemma of the paper, paraphrased]\label{lem:ind-rpt-cache}
During any run of the algorithm on an RPT $(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},\rho)$, every call
$\mathrm{solve}(A, \mathbf{x})$ returns exactly the value of the subquery of $Q$
rooted at $A$, conditioned on the part of the partial assignment $\mathbf{x}$ over the
variables of $A$; after the subsequent $\mathrm{fillCache}$ call, the cache $M_A$
stores exactly this value. Hence the top-level call
$\mathrm{solve}(r(\mathcal{P}), ())$ returns $Q(\mathbf{D})$. \eop
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Structural induction on the RPT, i.e.\ on the number of variables in the subtree
rooted at $A$. Base case (single variable): the call accumulates
$\bigoplus_{a} a$ directly, which is the subquery value. Step: the call iterates over
candidate values $a$, multiplies the --- by hypothesis correct --- returns of the
children's calls, and accumulates; the cache variant additionally uses that two partial
assignments agreeing on $A$'s interface define the same subquery value. (Full proof in
Section~\ref{sec:ind-appendix}.)
\end{proof}

The \emph{domination} half (every tree-decomposition plan is dominated by an RPT plan)
is leaf-peeling structural induction on the number of bags of the tree decomposition
$T$: with a single bag the plan is already a pseudo-tree plan (base case); otherwise
let $v$ be a leaf of $T$ with parent $p$, eliminate the variables occurring in $v$'s
bag but not in $p$'s into a single new atom over their joint neighbors, apply the
hypothesis to the smaller decomposition, and attach the resulting RPT at the parent ---
each peeling step preserves plan value and exponents, so the constructed RPT dominates.

\paragraph{What it buys.}
The optimizer gets a certified menu of space--time tradeoffs --- sublinear-space
evaluation of full queries by caching at the right granularity --- rather than a single
all-or-nothing algorithm. The cache invariant is the implementer's contract: it says
precisely what each cache entry means, so the recursion can be memoized (and its cost
accounted) safely; and the strict separations tell designers when adding a plan class
genuinely buys new exponent pairs.

\section{Near-duplicate text alignment by skyline sweeps}
\label{sec:ind-lsh}

The exemplar is a SIGMOD'26 paper on all-pair near-duplicate text alignment
\citep{sigmod26-153}. Finding all pairs of near-duplicate substrings in long texts is
a bread-and-butter entity-resolution task: plagiarism detection, version tracking,
deduplication of logs and records. Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) is the standard
sieve --- hash positions so that similar stretches collide, then verify candidates ---
but the candidate generation itself is the bottleneck: the maximal hash-consistent
stretches, which the paper calls \emph{tight intervals}, live in a space of
quadratically many intervals, and missing even one means a silently missed
near-duplicate pair. The paper characterizes tight intervals through a
\emph{dominance skyline} of positions and gives a monotone-stack sweep that generates
exactly the tight intervals of a text of length $n$ in $O(n)$ time per hash function,
with $O(\log n)$ expected stack --- and an $O(mn)$ expected-time alignment pipeline
with a completeness guarantee: no near-duplicate pair is missed.

\paragraph{Problem.}
Let $T$ be a text of length $n$ and $h$ a hash function on positions (drawn from a
random family); write $h(T[q])$ for the hash of the token at position $q$. Position
$q$ \emph{dominates} $p$ (for $p < q$) iff $h(T[q]) < h(T[p])$. An interval $[l,r]$ is
\emph{left-tight} iff $l = 1$ or $h(T[l-1])$ is smaller than every hash on $[l,r]$, and
\emph{right-tight} iff $r = n$ or $h(T[r+1])$ is smaller than every hash on $[l,r]$;
for a composite $g = (h_1,\dots,h_m)$, $[l,r]$ is tight iff it is left-tight under some
$h_i$ and right-tight under some $h_j$. The task: enumerate, exactly once each, all
tight intervals of $T$ under $h$ (and, for $m$ functions, under $g$).

\paragraph{Guarantee.}

\begin{theorem}[Tight-interval generation, Theorems 1--2 of the paper]\label{thm:ind-lsh}
The monotone-stack sweep (Algorithm 1 of the paper) generates exactly the tight
intervals of $T$ under $h$ --- every generated interval is tight, and every tight
interval is generated --- in $O(n)$ time, producing $O(n)$ intervals, with the stack
holding $O(\log n)$ positions in expectation. With $m$ independent hash functions the
full pipeline runs in $O(mn)$ expected time. \eop
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{How induction is applied.}
The induction variable is the scan position $r$; the state is the \emph{ordered
skyline} $S(r)$ --- the positions $p \le r$ not dominated by any later position, in
increasing order, whose hash values increase along the skyline and whose last element
is $r$. The invariant: at the end of each loop iteration, $S$ is exactly the ordered
skyline of the current position. The workhorse is the one-step update:

\begin{lemma}[Skyline update, Lemma 6 of the paper]\label{lem:ind-skyline}
Let $S(r) = (p_1, p_2, \dots, p_s)$ be the ordered skyline at position $r$ and
$i = \min\{\, j \in [1,s] : h(T[p_j]) < h(T[r+1]) \,\}$. Then the ordered skyline at
position $r+1$ is $S(r+1) = (p_1, \dots, p_i, r+1)$. \eop
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By the monotonicity of hashes along the skyline, every $p_j$ with $j > i$ has
$h(T[p_j]) > h(T[r+1])$, so the new position $r+1$ dominates exactly those --- they
leave the skyline. Positions are dominated only by later ones, so the prefix
$(p_1,\dots,p_i)$ keeps its pairwise order, and $r+1$ (the latest position, with the
largest hash on the new skyline) is appended last.
\end{proof}

The base case is $S(0) = (0)$. The stack operations --- pop while the top's hash
exceeds $h(T[r+1])$, then push $r+1$ --- implement exactly the update of
Lemma~\ref{lem:ind-skyline}, so the invariant holds after every iteration. Exactness
then follows by combining the invariant with two characterization lemmas of the paper:
an interval $[l,r]$ is left-tight iff $l-1 \in S(r) \setminus \{r\}$, and right-tight
iff $h(T[r+1]) < h(T[p])$ where $p$ is the smallest skyline position $\ge l$ (whose
hash is the minimum on $[l,r]$). The sweep emits an interval exactly at a pop, which
is exactly when both characterizations fire --- soundness and completeness at once.
(Full proof in Section~\ref{sec:ind-appendix}.)

\paragraph{What it buys.}
The alignment stage enumerates all LSH candidates in linear time per hash function,
replacing quadratic scans, with $O(\log n)$ expected working memory --- and the
exactness theorem doubles as a \emph{recall certificate} for the entity-resolution
pipeline: no near-duplicate pair can be missed. The pattern --- a dominance order on
positions, the skyline as state, a monotone stack as its compressed representation ---
is the same skeleton as classical monotone-stack algorithms, here made inductive and
analyzable.

\section{Succinct representations of all width-1 join plans}
\label{sec:ind-succinct}

The exemplar is a SIGMOD'26 paper on compressing the optimizer's search space
\citep{sigmod26-233}. Join-order selection is NP-hard in general, so cost-based
optimizers enumerate and cost candidate \emph{join trees}; for star queries the space
is enormous --- $n^{\,n-1}$ join trees for $n$ relations --- and enumerating them one
by one does not scale. The paper focuses on the plans that matter most,
\emph{width-1} plans whose intermediate results are bounded by $O(N)$ in the maximum
base cardinality $N$, and asks for a succinct representation from which all of them
can be enumerated without duplicates. Its headline results: width-1 plans exist
exactly for acyclic queries; a \emph{meta-decomposition} --- a labeled tree of size
$O(|E(H)|)$ computed in $O(|E(H)|^{3})$ --- represents all join trees of an acyclic
hypergraph; and an enumeration procedure outputs exactly the join trees, so the
optimizer searches a linear-size object instead of an exponential family.

\paragraph{Problem.}
Let $Q$ be a conjunctive query with hypergraph $H$ (edges = atoms). A \emph{join tree}
of $H$ is a tree over the hyperedges such that the hyperedges containing any vertex
form a connected subtree; $H$ is \emph{acyclic} iff it has a join tree. For a
(physical) query plan $P$, its \emph{width} $w(P)$ is the least exponent such that
every intermediate result of $P$ is $O(N^{w(P)})$. For a hyperedge $e$, its
\emph{outer part} is $o(e,H) = e \cap \bigl(\bigcup (E(H) \setminus \{e\})\bigr)$ ---
the portion of $e$ shared with other edges --- and an \emph{ear} is a hyperedge the
classical ear-removal characterization of acyclicity may peel off. The task:
characterize the width-1 plans and represent all join trees succinctly.

\paragraph{Guarantee.}

\begin{theorem}[Width-1 plans and their enumeration, Theorems of the paper,
compressed]\label{thm:ind-succinct}
(i) A conjunctive query $Q$ has a width-1 query plan if and only if $Q$ is acyclic.
(ii) For acyclic $H$, the meta-decomposition $M$ produced by the algorithm has
$|V(M)|, |E(M)| = O(|E(H)|)$, is computed in $O(|E(H)|^{3})$ time, and the enumeration
procedure outputs exactly the join trees of $H$ --- every output is a join tree, and
every join tree is output. \eop
\end{theorem}

\sidenote{The $n^{\,n-1}$ count for star queries is Cayley's formula --- the paper
pairs it with Pr\"ufer sequences to count join trees of specific shapes, sharpening
just how exponential the naive search space is.}

\paragraph{How induction is applied.}
The elimination structure is the ear-removal order: the meta-decomposition tree
records it (a node $p$ is a descendant of $q$ iff $e_p$ is removed before $e_q$), and
the correctness and completeness of the enumeration are proved by structural induction
over that tree --- the induction hypothesis at a node is that the subtree's physical
nodes induce a connected subtree of every join tree, so local choices extend to full
join trees and every join tree arises. The step case that lets the algorithm remove
ears in \emph{batches} is:

\begin{proposition}[Batch ear removal, Proposition of the paper]\label{prop:ind-ears}
Let $H$ be an acyclic hypergraph and $S = \{e_1, \dots, e_n\} \subseteq E(H)$,
$n \ge 2$, a set of distinct ears with a common outer part
$o(e_1,H) = \dots = o(e_n,H) =: o$. Then the hypergraph $H'$ with
$E(H') = (E(H) \setminus S) \cup \{o\}$ is again acyclic. \eop
\end{proposition}

The enabling fact --- which pins the shared structure of same-outer-part ears --- is a
two-line set-algebra lemma:

\begin{lemma}[Ears with equal outer parts, Lemma of the paper]\label{lem:ind-equal-outer}
If two ears $e_1, e_2$ of an acyclic hypergraph $H$ satisfy
$o(e_1,H) = o(e_2,H)$, then $o(e_1,H) = o(e_2,H) = e_1 \cap e_2$. \eop
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The common value lies in both ears (each outer part is contained in its own edge), and
conversely $e_1 \cap e_2$ lies in the outer part of $e_2$ because $e_1$ is one of
``the other edges'' for $e_2$ --- a double inclusion. (Full proof in
Section~\ref{sec:ind-appendix}.)
\end{proof}

With the batch proposition, the peeling induction proceeds on the number of ears
remaining: remove all same-outer-part ears at once, insert the single new ear $o$,
witness a join tree of $H'$ from one of $H$ (the shared outer part glues the removed
ears' positions into one subtree), and recurse on the strictly smaller hypergraph ---
the measure $|E(H)|$ decreases at every step.

\paragraph{What it buys.}
The optimizer keeps a linear-size certificate that stands for an exponential family of
plans: enumerating all width-1 --- i.e.\ linear-cost --- join plans becomes a traversal
of the meta-decomposition, with the exactness of the enumeration (no invalid plan, no
duplicates, nothing missed) inherited from the peeling induction. On top of it, the
paper evaluates relation-dominated queries in $O(|db|)$ time by restricting plan
search to the width-1 space the theorem certifies.

\section{Advanced usage and further reading}
\label{sec:ind-advanced}

The substrate lemmas of Section~\ref{sec:ind-tool} are complete --- nothing deeper is
ever added. What distinguishes advanced usage is the \emph{craft} on top: deliberate
strengthening of the hypothesis, and nesting one induction inside another. This
section states the strengthening principle, sketches two harder exemplars, and points
to the literature.

\subsection{The advanced rungs: strengthening and nesting}
\label{sec:ind-strengthening}

\begin{proposition}[Strengthening]\label{prop:ind-strengthen}
To certify a specification $P$ it suffices to exhibit \emph{any} inductive predicate
$I$ with $I \Rightarrow P$; the specification $P$ itself need not be inductive.
Formally: if $I(s_0)$, $I$ is preserved by $\Gamma$, and $I(s) \Rightarrow P(s)$ for
all states $s$, then $P$ holds at every reachable state. \eop
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:ind-invariant} applied to $I$ gives $I$ at every reachable state;
$I \Rightarrow P$ transfers it to $P$.
\end{proof}

The point is methodological. Target specifications are almost never inductive ---
``the output equals the subquery answer'' says nothing about a half-finished run ---
so the prover iterates: add bookkeeping to the predicate until the step case closes.
Every exemplar of this chapter did it: the exact block shape
(Lemma~\ref{lem:ind-elim-state}), the exact cache contents
(Lemma~\ref{lem:ind-rpt-cache}), the ordered skyline with its monotone hashes
(Lemma~\ref{lem:ind-skyline}), the ear-removal order with shared outer parts
(Proposition~\ref{prop:ind-ears}). \emph{Nesting} is the second rung: two induction
variables, an outer structural induction with an inner execution-step induction per
piece --- the sum--product exemplar of Section~\ref{sec:ind-sumprod} nests its
cache-invariant run argument inside the leaf-peeling domination proof. Beyond the
naturals, termination arguments move to well-founded measures on multisets; those live
in the term-rewriting literature rather than in this corpus.

\subsection{Two advanced exemplars, sketched}
\label{sec:ind-advanced-ex}

Both exemplars of this section push induction into proof machinery that is itself the
contribution; each is sketched in one paragraph.

\paragraph{Fixed-parameter width checking \citep{sigmod26-114}\pods.}
This PODS'26 paper settles the parameterized complexity of two central width measures:
it is fixed-parameter tractable to check $\mathrm{ghw}(H) \le k$ and
$\mathrm{fhw}(H) \le k$ --- generalised and fractional hypertree width, the
decomposition measures behind beyond-acyclic query evaluation --- parameterised by
$k$, the rank of $H$, and its maximum predicate arity $\Delta(H)$ (Corollary 8 of the
paper; the first FPT algorithms for these problems). The engine transplants the
MSO-transduction treewidth algorithm of Boja\'nczyk and Pilipczuk from tree
decompositions to \emph{elimination forests}: a transduction rewrites a suboptimal
forest into an optimal one, and its correctness is an induction over the forest whose
step case is the paper's Reduced-Hypergraph Dealternation Lemma --- alternation depth
(the \emph{split} and \emph{irregularity} parameters) stays bounded, with
conflict-graph coloring and neutral-swap/pigeonhole arguments supplying the bound. The
invariant carried by the induction: the output is always an elimination forest of the
same hypergraph, of width no larger than the input's.

\paragraph{Codd's theorem over semirings, and its limit \citep{sigmod26-290}.}
Codd's theorem --- relational algebra equals domain-independent relational calculus ---
is the foundation of declarative query languages, and provenance semirings (annotating
tuples with probabilities, counts, confidences) are the standard way to give queries
provenance semantics. The paper transports Codd's theorem to $\mathbb{K}$-relations
over semirings with \emph{monus} (truncated subtraction) and \emph{support}:
RA-expressibility, domain-independent calculus definability, and calculus definability
coincide exactly over zero-sum-free semirings (Theorem of the paper), and the
equivalence fails without zero-sum-freeness. Both directions rest on structural
induction on expressions and formulas: every algebra operator translates to a calculus
connective and back, and the hypothesis ``the translation defines the same
$\mathbb{K}$-relation'' is preserved by each operation. The same technique then proves
an \emph{inexpressibility}: over the bag semiring $\mathbb{N}$, division is not
definable in basic relational algebra. Witness databases make division produce a tuple
of multiplicity $2^{n}$, while an induction on the construction of any expression $E$
bounds its support by $n^{\,l(E)}$ and its multiplicities by $p_E(n) \cdot
2^{\,l(E)}$ --- growth that $2^{n}$ outruns. Induction here counts what expressions
\emph{can} do: the mirror image of its usual certifying use.

\subsection{Further reading}

\begin{itemize}
\item Floyd \citep{induction-bg1} and Hoare \citep{induction-bg2} --- the origins of
  the invariant-assertion method: verification as base case plus preservation.
\item Manna and Pnueli \citep{induction-bg3} --- safety properties (``nothing bad ever
  happens'') as the invariant-based fragment of temporal logic.
\item Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest, and Stein \citep{induction-bg4} --- the
  loop-invariant discipline for algorithms, with worked analyses.
\item Rosen \citep{induction-bg5} --- a refresher on strong induction, structural
  induction, and the well-ordering principle they rest on.
\end{itemize}

\begin{recap}
\item \textbf{What the tool is.} Induction converts claims about unbounded behavior
  into two finite checks --- an invariant holds initially and survives one step ---
  over whichever substrate the component offers: execution steps, recursive structure,
  or a decreasing measure (all three interderivable,
  Lemma~\ref{lem:ind-strong}).
\item \textbf{Which substrate when.} Explicit state machine (sweep, incremental
  index): state invariant. Recursively built object (decomposition, pseudo-tree,
  expression): structural induction, leaf-peeling by default. Process without explicit
  state (elimination order, rewrite sequence): strong induction on the natural
  measure.
\item \textbf{Why the craft matters.} Specifications are rarely inductive; strengthen
  the predicate --- exact block shape, exact cache contents, monotone skyline,
  removal order --- until the step case closes
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:ind-strengthen}), audit every operation, and pair the
  invariant with a decreasing measure to get termination and cost bounds.
\item \textbf{What the corpus showed.} Resistance-distance labels
  (Section~\ref{sec:ind-resistance}): induction on eliminated nodes, block-shape
  invariant. Sum--product plans (Section~\ref{sec:ind-sumprod}): cache invariant over
  the run, leaf-peeling over decompositions. LSH alignment
  (Section~\ref{sec:ind-lsh}): skyline invariant over the scan, monotone-stack update.
  Succinct plan representations (Section~\ref{sec:ind-succinct}): ear-removal
  induction with batch peeling.
\item \textbf{Advanced usage.} Strengthening and nesting
  (Section~\ref{sec:ind-strengthening}) are the rungs beyond the basics; the advanced
  exemplars of Section~\ref{sec:ind-advanced-ex} run induction inside MSO transductions
  and use it to count what expressions can do. Deferred proofs are collected in the
  Appendix (Section~\ref{sec:ind-appendix}).
\end{recap}

\section{Appendix}
\label{sec:ind-appendix}

Proofs omitted from the main text are collected here.

\subsubsection*{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:ind-elim-state} (elimination invariant)}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} After eliminating the nodes of
$U_2 \setminus U_1$ from the state initialized to $(L_{|U_2})^{-1}$, the state matrix
is $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} (L_{|U_1})^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$
--- zero rows and columns on eliminated nodes, the inverse restricted Laplacian on the
survivors.\eop

\begin{proof}
Induction on the number $k$ of eliminated nodes. \emph{Base case} $k = 0$: the state
is $S_0 = (L_{|U_2})^{-1}$ by initialization, and no rows or columns are zeroed.
\emph{Induction hypothesis}: after $k$ eliminations, all rows and columns of the
eliminated nodes are zero, and the block on the remaining node set $U$ is the inverse
of the Laplacian restricted to $U$. \emph{Step}: eliminate $v = v_{k+1}$ and partition
the state as $S_k = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} A & b \\ b^{\!\top} & c
\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ with $c = S_k[v,v]$. The rank-one update gives
\[
S_{k+1} \;=\; S_k - \frac{S_k[:,v]\,S_k[:,v]^{\!\top}}{c}
\;=\;
\begin{pmatrix} A - b\,b^{\!\top}/c & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},
\]
so row and column $v$ are zeroed. The surviving block $A - bb^{\!\top}/c$ is the Schur
complement of $c$ in $S_k$, and by the block-inverse identification --- if
$M = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} A' & B \\ C & D \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ has inverse
$\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} E & F \\ G & H \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, then
$E^{-1} = A' - BD^{-1}C$ --- the inverse of that block is the restriction of
$(S_k)^{-1}$ to the remaining nodes, which by the induction hypothesis is the Laplacian
restricted to $U \setminus \{v\}$. After all $|U_2 \setminus U_1|$ eliminations the
block on $U_1$ is $(L_{|U_1})^{-1}$.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:ind-rpt-cache} (RPT cache invariant)}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} Every call $\mathrm{solve}(A,\mathbf{x})$ returns
exactly the value of the subquery of $Q$ rooted at $A$ conditioned on the part of
$\mathbf{x}$ over $A$'s variables, and after $\mathrm{fillCache}$ the cache $M_A$
stores exactly this value; hence the top-level call returns $Q$.\eop

\begin{proof}
Write, as in the paper, $Q[Y \mid \mathbf{y}]$ for the subquery of $Q$ restricted to
the variables $Y$ and conditioned on the partial assignment $\mathbf{y}$, and let
$\chi(A)$ be the variables of the subtree rooted at $A$. Induction on
$|\chi(A)|$. \emph{Base case} $|\chi(A)| = 1$: the call allocates an output relation
and accumulates over the candidate values at $A$; by the paper's subquery identity
this accumulation is exactly $Q[\chi(A) \mid \mathbf{x}]$. \emph{Step}: for
$|\chi(A)| > 1$, the call iterates over candidates $a$ and multiplies the returns of
the children's calls,
\[
\mathrm{OUT} \;=\; \bigoplus_{a}\;
\Bigl( a \;\otimes\; \bigotimes_{B \in \mathrm{child}(A)}
\mathrm{solve}\bigl(B, (\mathbf{x},a)\bigr) \Bigr),
\]
which, by the induction hypothesis applied to the children (whose subtrees are
strictly smaller) and the same subquery identity, equals
$Q[\chi(A) \mid \mathbf{x}]$. For the cache clause, let $\mathbf{y}$ be the first
argument reaching $A$ whose restriction to $A$'s interface agrees with
$\mathbf{x}$'s: the call stores $Q[\chi(A) \mid \mathbf{y}]$ in $M_A$, and every later
call with the same interface returns it --- correct because two assignments agreeing
on the interface define equal subquery values,
$Q[\chi(A) \mid \mathbf{y}] = Q[\chi(A) \mid \mathbf{x}]$. The top-level call has an
empty assignment and the full tree, so it returns $Q$.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:ind-lsh} (tight-interval exactness)}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} The sweep generates exactly the tight intervals of
$T$: every generated interval is tight, every tight interval is generated; a text of
length $n$ is processed in $O(n)$ time with $O(n)$ intervals emitted and an
$O(\log n)$ expected stack.\eop

\begin{proof}
First, by induction on $r$: after the iteration processing $r$, the stack $S$ is the
ordered skyline $S(r)$. Base case $r = 0$: $S = (0)$. Step: the while-loop pops
exactly the positions whose hashes exceed $h(T[r+1])$, and the push appends $r+1$, so
by Lemma~\ref{lem:ind-skyline} the stack is $S(r+1)$. Second, soundness: the sweep
emits $[l,r]$ when popping $p$, where $l - 1$ is the new stack top, so
$l - 1 \in S(r) \setminus \{r\}$ and the paper's Lemma 1 gives left-tightness; the
popped $p$ is the smallest skyline position $\ge l$, its hash is the minimum on
$[l,r]$, and the pop condition $h(T[r+1]) < h(T[p])$ gives right-tightness by the
paper's Lemma 3. Third, completeness: fix a tight $[l,r]$. By Lemma 1,
$l - 1 \in S(r)$ at the start of iteration $r$; right-tightness gives
$h(T[r+1]) < h(T[p])$ for $p$ the smallest skyline position $\ge l$, so the pop of $p$
--- which the paper's Lemma 4 shows cannot be avoided --- exposes $l-1$ as the top,
and $[l,r]$ is emitted. For the complexity: each position is pushed once and popped at
most once, so the loop body executes $O(n)$ times and emits one interval per pop; the
stack bound is the paper's Lemma 7, $\mathbb{E}\,|S(r)| = O(\log r)$ over the random
hash family.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:ind-equal-outer} (equal outer parts)}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} If ears $e_1, e_2$ of an acyclic hypergraph $H$
satisfy $o(e_1,H) = o(e_2,H)$, then the common value is $e_1 \cap e_2$.\eop

\begin{proof}
Recall $o(e,H) = e \cap \bigl(\bigcup (E(H) \setminus \{e\})\bigr)$, so
$o(e,H) \subseteq e$ for every edge $e$. Let $o = o(e_1,H) = o(e_2,H)$. Since
$o = o(e_1,H) \subseteq e_1$ and $o = o(e_2,H) \subseteq e_2$, we get
$o \subseteq e_1 \cap e_2$. Conversely, $e_1$ is one of the other edges for $e_2$, so
\[
e_1 \cap e_2 \;\subseteq\; e_2 \cap \bigl(\bigcup (E(H) \setminus \{e_2\})\bigr)
\;=\; o(e_2,H) \;=\; o .
\]
The two inclusions give $o = e_1 \cap e_2$.
\end{proof}
