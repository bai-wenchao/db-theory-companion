\chapter{Amortized Analysis and Potential Functions}
\label{ch:amortized-potential}

Amortized analysis is the systems community's favorite certificate that
rebuilds, compression, merges, and splits are \emph{free}: a single operation
may detonate an $O(n)$ reorganization, but if many cheap operations must
accumulate before the next detonation becomes possible, the cost per operation
stays $O(1)$. Eleven papers in this corpus reach for the
amortized/potential toolbox, and they own one of the most lopsided
tool--property pairings in the survey: the pair
\emph{upper-bound} $\times$ \emph{amortized-potential} occurs in ten of the
eleven. The tool almost always arrives to certify a per-operation time or
space bound that an honest worst-case statement could not quote.

\section{The tool}

Three equivalent framings \cite{amortized-potential-bg3}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Aggregate method}: bound the total cost $T(n)$ of the worst
  sequence of $n$ operations and report $T(n)/n$.
\item \textbf{Accounting (banker's) method}: charge each operation a fictitious
  amortized price; cheap operations bank the surplus, expensive operations
  spend it, and the balance must never go negative.
\item \textbf{Potential (physicist's) method}: fix a function $\Phi$ from
  data-structure states to $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ and define the amortized cost
  $\hat{a}_i = c_i + \Phi(D_i) - \Phi(D_{i-1})$. Telescoping gives
  $\sum_i c_i \le \sum_i \hat{a}_i$ whenever $\Phi(D_n) \ge \Phi(D_0)$.
\end{itemize}

The design recipe for $\Phi$ has three canonical rules:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Nonnegativity.} $\Phi(D)\ge 0$ for every reachable state
  (typically $\Phi(D_0)=0$) --- this is what makes the telescoping inequality
  safe for every prefix of the sequence.
\item \textbf{Deposits.} Every cheap operation raises $\Phi$ by at most its
  amortized cost: $+O(1)$ per insertion into a counter, a slack variable, or a
  size-excess budget.
\item \textbf{Withdrawals.} Every expensive event consumes a \emph{large}
  accumulated $\Phi$: it can fire only once some measurable quantity
  (counter, excess, rounding slack) has crossed a threshold, and $\Phi$ then
  drops by at least the event's cost.
\end{enumerate}

Amortized bounds beat worst-case bounds exactly when expensive events are rare
\emph{because they require accumulation}: a worst-case bound must quote the
burst cost $C$ for every operation, while the amortized bound divides $C$ by
the $\Theta(\text{accrual})$ operations that had to precede it. The classic
showcases are dynamic tables, Fibonacci heaps, and splay trees, where $\Phi$
is a sum of imbalance terms
\cite{amortized-potential-bg1,amortized-potential-bg2}; periodic global
rebuilding is the degenerate special case ``rebuild cost over $\ell$'' for
period $\ell$ \cite{amortized-potential-bg5}. The signature recipe in this
corpus is \emph{rare-event bounding by a potential function}: a GK-style
quantile sketch holds at most $2s\ln(n/s+2)$ tuples because every compaction
spends accumulated error slack \cite{amortized-potential-bg4}, and a B-tree
root split needs amortized $\Omega(b^{h+2})$ insertions before it can recur.
The price of the method: the bound is a promise about \emph{sequences}, not
single operations --- latency- or adversary-sensitive settings require
deamortized variants.

\section{Five views from the corpus}

The five exemplars below exhibit progressively looser readings of the method:
an explicit sawtooth potential, an implicit batching argument, potential as
rounding slack, amortization imported as a black box, and potential as a
progress measure.

\subsection{SplineSketch: the sawtooth, literally}

A streaming quantile sketch maintains $k$ buckets over the keys seen so far,
each with a counter $b_i$ of absorbed items; arrivals increment counters, a
bucket whose counter violates its budget ($b_i > C_b\cdot n/k$) must
\emph{split}, and adjacent buckets are \emph{joined} to reclaim space. The
maintenance question is how many splits a stream of $n$ items can force.

\begin{lemma}[Split budget]
Combining the lower bound on the decrease due to a split with the upper bound
on the total potential, the number of splits during $(t',t]$ is at most
\[
\frac{t}{\beta\cdot C_b\cdot \frac{t}{2.5k}}
 \;=\; \frac{2.5\,k}{\beta\cdot C_b}\;\le\;\frac{k}{18},
\]
where $C_b$ is chosen based on $\beta$.
\end{lemma}

The potential is a function of the bucket counters --- their excess over the
budget $C_b\cdot n/k$: every arrival deposits $O(1)$, and the expensive event,
a split, consumes a constant fraction $\beta$ of what has accumulated, which is
literally the sawtooth of Figure~\ref{fig:amortized-sawtooth}. The split
budget then lets the rank error be charged along the split history, giving
$\varepsilon^{n}(\sigma_i)\le O(\log\alpha\cdot n/k)$ for any threshold
$\sigma_i$ of the final sketch \cite{sigmod26-363}.

\subsection{AeroSketch: batching the re-decomposition}

Tracking a covariance sketch of a row stream requires repeatedly extracting
the top eigenvectors of a $d\times d$ matrix --- far too expensive to redo on
every arrival.

\begin{theorem}
The algorithm solves the ATTP matrix-sketch problem with space complexity
$O(d\log\|A\|_F^{2})$ and amortized time complexity $O(d\log d)$ per update;
with probability amplification it achieves amortized time complexity
$O(d\log d\,\log^{2}\tfrac{1}{\delta})$ per update while guaranteeing success
probability at least $1-\delta$, where $\delta$ is a tunable parameter.
\end{theorem}

There is no explicit $\Phi$ here: the expensive event is the
eigen-decomposition of the accumulated covariance, executed by simultaneous
iteration in $O(d\log d)$ using $\log_2 d + 1$ power-iteration steps, and it
fires only when a dump threshold $\theta$ is crossed; each arriving row pays
its share of the burst, i.e.\ the amortized cost is burst$/\ell$ for batch
length $\ell$. A decomposability lemma --- per-site sketches compose
additively in covariance error --- then carries the same accounting to sliding
windows and distributed sites \cite{sigmod26-015}.

\subsection{Approximate query processing under updates: slack as potential}

For free-connex conjunctive queries over a semiring, exact incremental view
maintenance is blocked by dynamic lower bounds outside the tiny
q-hierarchical class. The escape is to keep auxiliary views only
approximately: each cached annotation is rounded up within a multiplicative
factor $b$ and refreshed lazily when the true value outgrows the cache.

\begin{theorem}
For any $b>1$ and any free-connex query $Q(H,F)$, all auxiliary views can be
updated in amortized $O(\log_b M)$ time under an insertion-only update
sequence, where $M=\max_{i,e}\max_{t\in R^{i}_{e}} A^{i}_{R_e}(t)$. Applied to
maintaining the join size of a 3-way join, which has $M=O(N^{2})$, the
amortized update cost is thus $O(\log_b N)$.
\end{theorem}

The potential is the total rounding slack $\sum_t \log_b A_R(t)$: an insertion
deposits $O(1)$, and the expensive event --- a cascading recomputation up the
view hierarchy, triggered when a tuple's annotation exceeds its cached value
by the factor-$b$ slack --- consumes a unit, so each tuple can fire at most
$\log_b M$ times. The insertion-only hypothesis is essential: fully-dynamic
sequences face an amortized $\Omega(\sqrt{N})$ lower bound for
non-q-hierarchical queries, and it holds even under approximation
\cite{sigmod26-274}.

\subsection{Subset sampling over joins: amortization imported}

One-shot and dynamic subset sampling over an acyclic join $Q$ must draw each
result tuple $u\in\mathrm{Join}(Q)$ independently with probability $p(u)$,
where $p$ factorizes over the relations of $Q$; the index shards tuples into
weight buckets --- each sub-instance is either light or near-uniform --- with a
meta-index steering rejection sampling across buckets.

\begin{theorem}
Given an acyclic join instance $Q$ and a set of associated weight functions
$\{p^{R}_{j}\}_{j\in Q}$, we can maintain a dynamic index under tuple
insertions using $O(N\log N)$ space, with amortized update time
$O(\log^{3} N\,\log\log N)$, while supporting the subset sampling query in
$O(1+\mu_{\Psi}\log N)$ expected time, where $N$ is the total number of
insertions.
\end{theorem}

The expensive events are index rebalancing and the periodic re-derivation of
per-bucket statistics as insertions shift tuples across weight classes; the
amortized $O(\log N)$ maintenance primitive is imported rather than re-proved
(the paper's Lemma~5.1, from prior work: the direct-access join index over
real and dummy tuples updates in amortized $O(\log N)$ per inserted tuple).
The composability lesson: a data structure with an amortized guarantee can
serve as a black-box component inside a larger amortized analysis
\cite{sigmod26-232}\pods

\subsection{Jaguar: potential as progress measure}

Jaguar evaluates conjunctive queries in submodular-width time by recursively
decomposing the instance; at each node, \textsc{Calibrate} re-derives size
bounds $g(Z)$ for subsets $Z$ of the query variables --- in fact a
shortest-path computation on a weighted graph whose vertices are the subsets
of $V$.

\begin{theorem}
Let $\varepsilon>0$ be an arbitrary constant. Given a CQ $Q$, a set of
statistics $(\Sigma,n)$, and a database instance $D_0$ that matches the schema
of $Q$ and satisfies the statistics $(\Sigma,n)$, Jaguar correctly computes
$Q(D_0)$ in time $O(N^{\mathrm{subw}(Q,\Sigma,n)+\varepsilon}+\mathrm{OUT})$
in data complexity, where $N=|D_0|$ and $\mathrm{OUT}$ is the output size. In
particular, after a preprocessing time of
$O(N^{\mathrm{subw}(Q,\Sigma,n)+\varepsilon})$, Jaguar supports constant-delay
enumeration of the output tuples.
\end{theorem}

Here the potential is not a bank but a \emph{progress measure}: the
log-relation-size bound $g$. Light recursion edges leave the statistics intact
(a light path has length at most $2^{|V|}=O(1)$), while each heavy edge
decreases the potential by at least $\varepsilon$ --- so any root-to-leaf path
carries at most $O(1)$ heavy edges and the recursion tree has $O(1)$ depth in
data complexity. The expensive event is the heavy, statistics-tightening
recursive call: it can fire only while potential remains to burn, and with
linear-time \textsc{Calibrate} plus the invariant
$c\le\mathrm{subw}(Q,\Sigma,n)$ on every calibrated bound, no level of the
constant-depth tree exceeds the advertised
$N^{\mathrm{subw}(Q,\Sigma,n)+\varepsilon}$ \cite{sigmod26-141}\pods

\begin{reachbox}{When to reach for amortized analysis}
\begin{itemize}
\item You need a \emph{per-operation} time or space bound, and the algorithm
  has an occasional expensive structural event --- split, merge, rebuild,
  compression, re-decomposition, full scan --- that only many cheap operations
  can set up.
\item The trigger is measurable: some counter, excess, or slack that rises by
  $O(1)$ per cheap operation and drops sharply when the event fires. If you
  can write it as a $\Phi$ obeying the three rules, the bound follows by
  telescoping --- and you get to tune the regime (batch length $\ell$, slack
  ratio $b$, bucket budget $C_b$) to trade burst size against frequency.
\item Sequences, not single operations: if the deployment cares about tail
  latency or faces an adversary who inspects timings, budget for deamortized
  variants; and remember that insert-only analyses can shatter under deletions
  (the $\Omega(\sqrt{N})$ fully-dynamic lower bound above).
\end{itemize}
\end{reachbox}
