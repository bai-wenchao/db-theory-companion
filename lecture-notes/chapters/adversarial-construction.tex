\begin{objectives}
\item Recognize the four targets of an impossibility result: cost, expressivity, output size, update scope.
\item Build the three plants: an instance pair, a hard distribution, a witness object.
\item Match the pattern to the target; import adversaries via reductions when direct planting fails.
\item Dissect a lower-bound section: model, family, indistinguishability, contradiction.
\end{objectives}

An upper bound is a promise; an impossibility result is the proof that no better promise exists.
The second kind of theorem is not derived from the first --- it is \emph{built}. To show that no
algorithm, language, or index in a model can do better, the author constructs the world in which
every allowable procedure fails: a pair of instances that look identical, a distribution of
inputs hiding a secret, a stream of updates engineered so that staying correct is provably
expensive, a witness object so large that merely reporting it exceeds the budget. That engineered
world is the \emph{adversary}, and building it is a craft with a small number of recurring
patterns. In the SIGMOD'26 corpus the tool is used by 22 tagged papers, and it owns the survey's
signature pairing: in 13 of them the same article proves an upper bound \emph{and} the
adversarial lower bound that matches it. The pairing is no accident --- the upper bound tells you
what the adversary must force; the adversary certifies that the upper bound is the end of the
line.

This chapter presents the basic patterns and the standard exemplars first;
Section~\ref{sec:adv-advanced} adds the advanced regimes (adversaries against computational
indistinguishability and against structural properties) as extension reading.

\section{The tool: adversarial constructions}
\label{sec:adv-tool}

The problem class is uniform across applications. There is a \emph{model} $\mathcal{M}$ --- an
observation interface (queries into a graph, a stream seen once, a sketch, the syntax of an
algebra), a class of permitted procedures, and a cost measure (queries, time, space, edges,
update work) --- and a task $f$ with a required accuracy. The deliverable is a theorem of the
form ``no procedure in $\mathcal{M}$ achieves $f$ within resource $R$,'' and its proof always
exhibits an explicit counter-world: the adversary. What varies from paper to paper is only
\emph{what all procedures are forced to share} --- an identical observation, an identical
data-structure state, a bounded growth ceiling, or an output that is simply too large --- and
that invariant is the organizing question of this chapter.

\begin{remarkbox}[Notation]
``Adversary'' always means a construction fixed \emph{before} the algorithm's coins: an instance
pair, an input distribution, or an update sequence. A lower bound of $\Omega(g(n))$ is a
statement about every procedure in the model; a \emph{conditional} lower bound holds under a
stated conjecture. $\Phi$ denotes an aspect ratio (largest over smallest nonzero distance),
$\lambda$ a doubling dimension, $\delta_G$ the degeneracy of a graph $G$.
\end{remarkbox}

\subsection{The basic patterns}
\label{sec:adv-patterns}

Every adversarial argument in the corpus is one of five patterns, stated here in their bare
forms. The first is the atom from which the others are assembled.

\begin{definition}[Observation model and transcripts]\label{def:adv-transcript}
An \emph{observation model} assigns to each instance $I$ and each (possibly randomized)
algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ the \emph{transcript} $\tau_{\mathcal{A}}(I)$: the complete record of
what $\mathcal{A}$ observes on $I$, including its own coins. Instances $I_0, I_1$ are
\emph{indistinguishable}, written $\tau(I_0) \equiv \tau(I_1)$, if for every procedure permitted
by the model the distributions of $\tau_{\mathcal{A}}(I_0)$ and $\tau_{\mathcal{A}}(I_1)$
coincide.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Two-instance lower bound]\label{prop:adv-indist}
Let $f$ be a task solved with tolerance $\theta$ (the answer must fall within $\theta$ of
$f(I)$). If there exist instances $I_0, I_1$ with $\tau(I_0) \equiv \tau(I_1)$ but
$|f(I_0) - f(I_1)| > 2\theta$, then no algorithm in the model solves $f$. \eop
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On the shared observation, the algorithm's output distribution is the same for both instances.
Whatever value $v$ it emits with positive probability, $v$ cannot simultaneously lie within
$\theta$ of $f(I_0)$ and within $\theta$ of $f(I_1)$; hence on one of the two instances the
algorithm errs with positive probability.
\end{proof}

\sidenote{The tolerance in Proposition~\ref{prop:adv-indist} is where approximation parameters
enter: planting instances whose answers differ by just over $2\theta$ is exactly how
$\varepsilon$'s in lower bounds are beaten --- the gap must exceed the promised accuracy, not
merely be nonzero.}

Randomized algorithms do not escape the pattern --- they route it through a distribution.
\emph{Yao's minimax principle} \citep{adversarial-construction-bg1} says that derandomizing the
\emph{input}, not the algorithm, loses nothing:

\begin{theorem}[Yao's minimax principle]\label{thm:adv-yao}
For any cost function $\operatorname{cost}(\mathcal{A}, x) \ge 0$,
\[
\min_{\text{randomized } \mathcal{R}}\;\; \max_{x}\;
  \mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{cost}(\mathcal{R}, x)\bigr]
\;\ge\;
\max_{\text{distributions } \mathcal{D}}\;\; \min_{\text{deterministic } \mathcal{A}}\;
  \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\operatorname{cost}(\mathcal{A}, x)\bigr]. \eopm
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix any $\mathcal{D}$ and any randomized $\mathcal{R}$. Averaging over the coins of
$\mathcal{R}$ first,
$\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{cost}(\mathcal{R},x)]
= \mathbb{E}_{\text{coins}}\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}
[\operatorname{cost}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{coins}},x)]
\ge \min_{\mathcal{A}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[\operatorname{cost}(\mathcal{A},x)]$.
The left side is at most $\max_x \mathbb{E}[\operatorname{cost}(\mathcal{R},x)]$; minimize over
$\mathcal{R}$, then maximize over $\mathcal{D}$.
\end{proof}

So against a randomized algorithm one exhibits a \emph{hard distribution} and analyzes the best
deterministic procedure against it --- which is why so many lower bounds in the corpus are
phrased as ``draw the input from $\Sigma$; any estimator with $T$ queries fails'' (the pattern
of Section~\ref{sec:adv-ppr}).

The next two patterns need no planting at all: the adversary \emph{is} an object.

\begin{proposition}[Witness counting]\label{prop:adv-output}
If some instance $I$ of size $n$ has an output set $\operatorname{Out}(I)$ with
$|\operatorname{Out}(I)| \ge g(n)$, and a correct algorithm must report every element of
$\operatorname{Out}(I)$, then every correct algorithm takes $\Omega(g(n))$ time. \eop
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The algorithm must write $g(n)$ distinct answers; writing is a lower bound on running time.
\end{proof}

The classical witness is Moon--Moser's \citep{adversarial-construction-bg4}: a graph on $n$
vertices with $3^{n/3}$ maximal cliques, each of which an enumerator must output. The pattern
reappears with a defect budget in Section~\ref{sec:adv-defclique}, where the witness is
generalized to $k$-defective cliques and certifies an enumeration algorithm as worst-case
optimal.

When the object under attack is a \emph{language} rather than a cost, the shared invariant is a
growth ceiling: everything expressible in the model is small in some measure, and the target
operation is not.

\begin{proposition}[Growth ceiling]\label{prop:adv-ceiling}
Let $\mu \ge 0$ be a measure on instances such that every expression of the model with
description length $\ell$, applied to inputs of size $n$, produces outputs with
$\mu \le n^{O(\ell)} \cdot c^{\ell}$ for a constant $c$ of the model. If the target operation
$T$ has, for every fixed $\ell$, an input $I$ with $\mu(T(I)) > n^{O(\ell)} \cdot c^{\ell}$
(for instance $\mu(T(I)) = 2^{|I|}$), then no expression of the model defines $T$. \eop
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Any candidate defining expression has some fixed length $\ell$; pick $n$ large enough that the
demanded $\mu$-value exceeds its ceiling $n^{O(\ell)} c^{\ell}$, which exists because $2^n$
outgrows every polynomial in $n$ times a constant. The expression's output is then too small
in $\mu$ to equal $T(I)$.
\end{proof}

\sidenote{The corpus instance: on $\mathbb{N}$-databases, every expression of basic relational
algebra produces multiplicities at most polynomial in the input size times a factor exponential
only in the expression's own length, while division demands multiplicities $2^n$ --- so
division is inexpressible \citep{sigmod26-290}. Expressivity counterexamples of this kind go
back to Codd \citep{adversarial-construction-bg6}. No pair of instances is needed; growth rates
alone separate.}

Finally, when no direct planting suffices, the adversary is \emph{imported}: a reduction
converts instances of a believed-hard problem into instances of yours.

\begin{proposition}[Reduction transfer]\label{prop:adv-reduction}
Suppose instances of a problem $H$, conjectured to require $T_H(n)$ time, map in
$o(T_H(n))$ time to instances of $P$ of size at most $s(n)$. Then any algorithm for $P$
running in $T(n)$ time with $T(s(n)) = o(T_H(n))$ refutes the conjecture; equivalently, $P$
requires $\Omega\bigl(T_H(s^{-1}(n))\bigr)$ time under the conjecture. \eop
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Compose the reduction with the algorithm: an instance of $H$ of size $n$ becomes an instance of
$P$ of size $\le s(n)$, solved in $T(s(n)) + o(T_H(n)) = o(T_H(n))$ time, contradicting the
conjecture.
\end{proof}

The conjecture is usually a fine-grained one --- combinatorial $k$-clique, OuMv
\citep{adversarial-construction-bg5}, SETH --- and the reduction is itself an adaptive
adversary: it manufactures the update stream, and indistinguishability is inherited from the
source problem rather than planted directly. When the source is a communication problem
instead, the two-instance pattern arrives prefabricated: index, set-disjointness, and
gap-Hamming transcripts are information-blind by construction
\citep{adversarial-construction-bg2}, the route by which sketching lower bounds entered
databases with the frequency-moment bound \citep{adversarial-construction-bg3}.

\subsection{How to choose the pattern}
\label{sec:adv-choose}

The selection is mechanical once two questions are answered. \emph{First, what is the resource
under attack?} If it is running time or query complexity, ask whether the model restricts
observation (a sketch, a query interface, one pass over a stream): it does $\to$ plant two
instances or a hard distribution; it does not (arbitrary computation allowed) $\to$ import
hardness via a reduction, since nothing else stops an unrestricted algorithm. If the resource
is the \emph{output}, use a witness object. If it is \emph{expressivity}, use a growth ceiling
or a symmetry. If it is \emph{update work} in a dynamic setting, the shared observation is the
data structure's state and the instances are futures of the update stream.
\emph{Second, is the algorithm randomized?} Then Yao
(Theorem~\ref{thm:adv-yao}) converts your task to exhibiting one hard distribution and beating
the best deterministic procedure on it.

\begin{reachbox}{When to Build an Adversary}
Reach whenever the deliverable is a no-better-is-possible statement --- certifying an algorithm
worst-case optimal, showing an index's edge budget cannot shrink, separating a language
fragment, proving an update operation cannot stay local --- or when planning an upper bound and
needing to know what the adversary will force. Then run the recipe:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Fix the model precisely: what procedures may observe, what they must output, the
  tolerance $\theta$, the cost measure. Sloppy models make sloppy impossibilities.
\item Identify what \emph{all} procedures must share: a transcript, a state, a growth ceiling,
  an output set. This shared object is where the adversary lives.
\item Plant the two instances (Proposition~\ref{prop:adv-indist}), a hard distribution
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:adv-yao}), a witness object
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:adv-output}), or a ceiling-breaking instance
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:adv-ceiling}); if nothing plants, import via a reduction
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:adv-reduction}).
\item Tune the parameters so the shared observation is exactly identical (not merely similar)
  while the demanded answers differ by more than $2\theta$; verify no adaptive strategy can
  break the symmetry.
\item If every two-instance attempt fails, take the hint: the matching upper bound may simply
  exist. Try to prove it before fighting the lower bound further.
\end{enumerate}
\end{reachbox}

\section{Defective clique enumeration: the output as adversary}
\label{sec:adv-defclique}

The exemplar is a SIGMOD'26 paper on relaxed clique computation \citep{sigmod26-306}. Exact
cliques are famously brittle: one absent edge destroys one, so real communities --- friendship
groups, protein complexes, fraud rings --- are better modeled by \emph{defective} cliques,
vertex sets that miss at most $k$ edges for a small budget $k$. The paper studies enumerating
all \emph{maximal} $k$-defective cliques and searching for large ones, the way a graph engine
would when $k$-hop tolerance matters. Its design question is classical branch-and-bound
craft --- how to pivot, how to prune --- but its headline is a worst-case optimality guarantee:
the search tree has at most $O(3^{n/3}\cdot n^k)$ nodes for constant $k$, and this is optimal
because no algorithm can beat the size of the output it must produce. The adversary here is not
a pair of instances at all: it is a single witness graph, generalized from Moon--Moser's, whose
maximal defective cliques are so numerous that enumerating them dominates any analysis. The
paper complements the witness with degeneracy-aware bounds that make the algorithm practical on
sparse graphs.

\paragraph{Problem.}
Given a graph $G=(V,E)$ with $n$ vertices and an integer $k \ge 0$, a vertex subset
$S \subseteq V$ is a \emph{$k$-defective clique} if $G[S]$ misses at most $k$ edges, i.e.\ has
at least $\binom{|S|}{2} - k$ edges. $S$ is \emph{maximal} if no superset is $k$-defective. The
task is to enumerate all maximal $k$-defective cliques; the cost measure is the number of
search-tree nodes (and total time) of any branching algorithm, judged against
$\max_G |\operatorname{Out}(G)|$, the worst-case output size.

\sidenote{Every $k$-defective clique of size $q$ contains a clique of size $q-k$ --- delete one
endpoint of each missing edge. So the defective budget interpolates between clique enumeration
($k=0$, Moon--Moser territory) and densest-subgraph-style relaxation.}

\paragraph{Guarantee.}

\begin{theorem}[Worst-case optimal enumeration, combining the search-space and output-size
theorems of the paper]\label{thm:adv-defclique}
For constant $k$, the worst-case output size of maximal $k$-defective clique enumeration is
$\Omega(3^{n/3}\cdot n^k)$: there is a graph on $n$ vertices with that many maximal
$k$-defective cliques. The paper's branching algorithm enumerates them all with a search tree
of $O(3^{n/3}\cdot n^k)$ nodes in $O(m\cdot 3^{n/3}\cdot n^k)$ time, and its degeneracy-aware
variant runs in $O\bigl(n\cdot 3^{\delta_G/3}\cdot \delta_G^{O(k)}\bigr)$ time. \eop
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{How the adversary is built.}
The witness is the \emph{Moon--Moser graph} $M_n$: the complete $(n/3)$-partite graph whose
parts have size $3$ (equivalently, the complement of $n/3$ disjoint triangles)
\citep{adversarial-construction-bg4}. The $k=0$ case is the classical count --- one vertex per
part gives $3^{n/3}$ maximal cliques --- and the defective budget $k$ multiplies the harvest by
a polynomial factor:

\begin{lemma}[Witness count for defective cliques; output lemma of the paper, notation
cleaned]\label{lem:adv-mmcount}
For $n$ a multiple of $3$ with $n/3 \ge k$, the Moon--Moser graph $M_n$ contains at least
$3^{n/3}\cdot\binom{n/3}{k} = \Omega(3^{n/3}\cdot n^k)$ maximal $k$-defective cliques. \eop
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Choose $k$ of the $n/3$ parts; inside each chosen part keep \emph{both} endpoints of one of its
$3$ vertex pairs (one missing edge), and in every unchosen part keep a single vertex. The
resulting set misses exactly $k$ edges --- one per chosen part --- and is maximal because the
only addable vertices are the third vertices of chosen parts, each of which would create a
second missing edge in its part. Counting the choices gives
$\binom{n/3}{k}\cdot 3^{k} \cdot 3^{\,n/3-k}$ distinct maximal $k$-defective cliques. (Full
proof in Section~\ref{sec:adv-appendix}.)
\end{proof}

Every maximal $k$-defective clique must appear in the enumeration, so
Proposition~\ref{prop:adv-output} converts the witness count into the time lower bound of
Theorem~\ref{thm:adv-defclique} --- and the algorithm's $O(3^{n/3} n^k)$ search tree matches it,
with no gap. The upper-bound side then does the delicate work: a pivoting rule for defective
cliques (every maximal solution through a partial set must touch the non-neighborhood of a
pivot) and a branching-vector induction showing the tree size never exceeds the output bound,
which is where the $n^k$ factor is earned rather than assumed.

\paragraph{What it buys.}
The engine gets a certificate that its enumeration cost is a property of the problem, not of
the algorithm: no competitor can be asymptotically faster at worst case. The degeneracy-aware
bound turns the same analysis into a practical promise --- on sparse real graphs the exponent
collapses from $n/3$ to $\delta_G/3$ --- and the witness graph itself becomes the natural
stress test for any implementation claiming output sensitivity.

\section{Proximity graphs: point sets that force edges}
\label{sec:adv-proxgraph}

The exemplar is a SIGMOD'26 paper on proximity graphs for similarity search
\citep{sigmod26-349}. Vector-database indexes increasingly build a \emph{proximity graph} over
the point set and answer nearest-neighbor queries by greedy navigation: from any start point,
repeatedly hop to the out-neighbor closest to the query, and stop when no neighbor is closer.
The paper asks the quantitative question behind every such index: how many \emph{edges} must a
graph carry so that greedy navigation always lands on a $(1+\varepsilon)$-approximate nearest
neighbor? It answers both directions. Positively, in $d$-dimensional Euclidean space a
$(1+\varepsilon)$-proximity graph with $O((1/\varepsilon)^{\lambda}\cdot n)$ edges --- linear in
$n$ --- exists and can be built in near-linear time with high probability, where $\lambda$ is
the doubling dimension. Negatively, in general doubling metrics no such sparsity is possible:
there are point sets on which \emph{any} $(1+\varepsilon)$-proximity graph, no matter how much
query time it is allowed, must carry nearly $n\log\Phi$ edges, where $\Phi$ is the aspect ratio
of the point set. The lower bound is the paper's separation theorem: it shows the Euclidean
assumption is not cosmetic --- drop it and the edge budget necessarily blows up by a factor
$\log\Phi$.

\paragraph{Problem.}
Let $P$ be $n$ points in a metric space with aspect ratio $\Phi$ (largest over smallest nonzero
pairwise distance) and doubling dimension $\lambda$. A directed graph $G$ on $P$ is a
\emph{$(1+\varepsilon)$-proximity graph} ($(1+\varepsilon)$-PG) if, for every query point $q$
and every start vertex, greedy navigation --- hop to the out-neighbor nearest to $q$ ---
terminates at a $(1+\varepsilon)$-approximate nearest neighbor of $q$ in $P$. The task is to
bound the minimum number of edges of a $(1+\varepsilon)$-PG over $P$; a ``2-PG'' is the case
$(1+\varepsilon)=2$.

\sidenote{\emph{Doubling dimension} $\lambda$: every ball of radius $2r$ is coverable by
$2^{\lambda}$ balls of radius $r$; it is the standard notion of intrinsic dimension for metric
indexes. Navigability and $(1+\varepsilon)$-PG-hood are equivalent in the paper's formulation,
so the bound is about graphs, not about any particular search procedure.}

\paragraph{Guarantee.}

\begin{theorem}[Size lower bound, Statement 1 of the paper's lower-bound theorem, notation
cleaned]\label{thm:adv-proxlb}
For any powers of two $n \ge 2$ and $\Phi$ with $n^2 \le \Phi \le 2^n$, there is a set $P$ of
$\Theta(n)$ points with aspect ratio $\Phi$, in a metric space of doubling dimension $1$, such
that any 2-PG for $P$ must have $\Omega(n\log\Phi)$ edges, regardless of the query time
allowed. \eop
\end{theorem}

A companion statement extends the bound to a range of $\varepsilon$: with $n = s^d\cdot t$
points, doubling dimension at most $\log(1+2^d)$ and $\varepsilon = 1/(2s)$, any
$(1+\varepsilon)$-PG needs $\Omega(s^d\cdot n)$ edges --- again independent of query time.

\paragraph{How the adversary is built.}
The adversary is a planted point set in a tree-like metric --- doubling dimension $1$ is the
regime where every ball splits into two half-radius balls, and the construction exploits
exactly that binary hierarchy across the $\log\Phi$ distance scales spanned by the aspect
ratio. The mechanism is \emph{scale separation}: the two-instance pattern of
Proposition~\ref{prop:adv-indist} applied once per distance scale. The witness spans
$\log\Phi$ scales, and a navigable graph must support greedy descent through every one of
them: at each scale the construction places groups of points whose relevant distances sit at
that scale, while an edge that could serve one scale cannot serve another because consecutive
scales differ by more than the stretch factor. The necessity counting runs per scale and
yields a linear edge count for each; summing over the $\log\Phi$ scales gives
$\Omega(n\log\Phi)$ (the paper's proof develops the counting in detail; we sketch the
architecture). Two features make the bound robust. First, it is
\emph{non-geometric}: the witness metric is a hierarchy, not a point set in any
$\mathbb{R}^d$, which is precisely why it does not contradict the Euclidean upper bound ---
the separation between general doubling metrics and Euclidean space is the theorem's point.
Second, the counted quantity is a property of the \emph{object} (the edge set of any graph
that is navigable at all), not of a procedure's cost, which is what the ``regardless of the
query time allowed'' clause buys: no amount of walk-length tolerance dodges an edge count. The
generalization is honest work, as the paper notes: for $\varepsilon$-dependence
(Statement 2), the hard input extends a classical query-time lower-bound witness for
approximate nearest-neighbor data structures, but lifting a query-time witness to a
\emph{size} witness demands new ideas, since the adversary must now defeat graphs that may be
arbitrarily dense in the wrong places.

\paragraph{What it buys.}
For index builders the theorem is a floor: on skewed-distance data (large aspect ratio), a
proximity-graph index with near-linear edges simply does not exist in general metrics, so
practitioners either exploit Euclidean structure or pay $\log\Phi$. The upper bound's linear-in-$n$
Euclidean graph with near-linear construction closes the design space from above, and the pair
of results together tells system designers exactly which assumption --- doubling dimension plus
geometry versus doubling dimension alone --- their edge budget depends on.

\section{Bi-core maintenance: an update pair that lies in wait}
\label{sec:adv-bicore}

The exemplar is a SIGMOD'26 paper on maintaining bipartite cores \citep{sigmod26-179}.
Bipartite graphs model user--item, author--paper, and customer--product relations, and their
\emph{bi-cores} are the standard cohesion notion: the $(\alpha,\beta)$-core is the largest
subgraph in which every vertex on the left has degree at least $\beta$ and every vertex on the
right at least $\alpha$. When edges arrive and depart, a system wants to maintain all bi-core
memberships without recomputing from scratch. The paper's first contribution is an asymmetry
theorem explaining why this is hard in one direction only: under the model of
\emph{locally persistent} algorithms --- the model prior maintenance algorithms implicitly
operate in, where an update is processed by examining local structure around the affected
edge --- handling \emph{deletions} is bounded (work proportional to what actually changes),
while handling \emph{insertions} is unbounded. Its second contribution is a family of
order-based algorithms that exploit the asymmetry: a maintained vertex order with
``lack values'' gives necessary conditions pruning which vertices can possibly change, taming
insertion in practice.

\paragraph{Problem.}
Let $G = (V_1, V_2, E)$ be a bipartite graph; for a pair $(\alpha,\beta)$, the
\emph{$(\alpha,\beta)$-core} is the maximal subgraph where every $v \in V_1$ has degree
$\ge \beta$ and every $v \in V_2$ has degree $\ge \alpha$; each vertex belongs to a set of
\emph{c-pairs}. Under single-edge insertions and deletions, maintain all c-pairs. An update
algorithm is \emph{locally persistent} (LP) if its processing of an update is confined to the
persistent local information around the affected vertices; it is \emph{bounded} if its cost per
update is a function of the true change set $\Delta R$ alone --- in particular $O(1)$ on
updates that change no core membership.

\sidenote{The LP model abstracts what deployed maintenance algorithms actually do: identify
candidate c-pairs near the edge, expand a candidate set, and update --- never recomputing
globally. ``Unbounded'' then means: there are updates where the algorithm must do
$\omega(|\Delta R|)$ work, i.e.\ where local examination cannot certify that nothing changed.}

\paragraph{Guarantee.}

\begin{theorem}[Insertion--deletion asymmetry, Theorems~3.1--3.3 of the paper]
\label{thm:adv-bicore}
(i) Given a bipartite graph $G$ and all its bi-cores, updating them after inserting an edge
$(u,v)$ is \emph{unbounded} under the model of locally persistent algorithms: some updates
force $\omega(1)$ work while the true change set is empty.
(ii) Updating the bi-cores after deleting an edge $(u,v)$ is \emph{bounded}, in time
$O\bigl(|\textsf{CHANGED}| + |N_1(\textsf{CHANGED})|\bigr)$ for the natural deletion procedure.
\eop
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{How the adversary is built.}
The adversary is the two-instance pattern wearing dynamic clothes
(Figure~\ref{fig:adv-twoinstance}): the shared observation is the fully processed state of
$G$, and the two instances are its two futures. The construction is distilled in the paper's
workhorse lemma.

\begin{lemma}[The two-update trap, proof of Theorem~3.1 of the paper, notation cleaned]
\label{lem:adv-twowupdates}
There is a bipartite graph $G$ with $n$ vertices and two single-edge insertions
$\Delta G_1, \Delta G_2$ such that: (i) for each of $\Delta G_1$ and $\Delta G_2$ alone the true
change set $\Delta R$ is empty; (ii) applied in sequence, $|\Delta R| = \Omega(n)$. Hence any
LP algorithm that is bounded spends $O(1)$ on either update alone, yet any algorithm that
maintains correctness must spend $\Omega(\ell)$ work across the two updates for a non-constant
$\ell$ --- a contradiction. \eop
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Build $G$ so that each insertion is individually harmless --- neither moves any vertex across
a core threshold by itself --- but the two jointly push a cascade of $\Omega(n)$ vertices over
their thresholds. A bounded LP algorithm must return in $O(1)$ time after the first update
(since $\Delta R = \emptyset$ certifies there is nothing to do), leaving its local state
unprepared; when the second update arrives, correctness forces the full cascade to be
processed, which cannot be charged to either update's change set. (The paper's complete
construction appears in its extended version.)
\end{proof}

The trap is exactly Proposition~\ref{prop:adv-indist} with $\tau$ = the algorithm's internal
state after processing $G$: the two futures $\Delta G_1$ and $\Delta G_2$ are locally
indistinguishable (each looks like a no-op), so the algorithm must behave identically on both
--- but the demanded maintenance work differs, and one of the two futures punishes it.

\paragraph{What it buys.}
The theorem tells core-maintenance systems that insertion cannot be both local and
worst-case bounded --- explaining the observed bottleneck (long BFS expansions) in prior
algorithms, which the paper measures at dominating proportions of update time. The fix it
motivates is the order-based design: since boundedness is impossible in general, prune with
necessary conditions (an update can cascade only along monotone paths in a maintained order,
and only through vertices whose ``lack values'' hit zero), reducing practical insertion cost
to $O\bigl(|N_1(\textsf{AFF})| \cdot |N_2(\textsf{AFF})|\bigr)$ over the actually affected
sets.

\section{Personalized PageRank: hard distributions with hidden splits}
\label{sec:adv-ppr}

The exemplar is a PODS'26 paper on single-source personalized PageRank \citep{sigmod26-166}\pods.
Personalized PageRank (PPR) asks where a random surfer who teleports back to a source $s$ with
probability $\alpha$ at every step will land: the score $\pi(s,t)$ powers recommendation,
ranking, and graph mining over large graphs, and systems from web search to graph databases
estimate it millions of times a day. The single-source task estimates the whole vector
$\pi(s,\cdot)$ at once, with two standard accuracies --- relative error for the coordinates
above a threshold $\delta$, or additive error $\varepsilon$ --- and cost measured in
\emph{queries}: the paper works in the arc-centric model, where the algorithm may ask for the
$k$-th in- or out-edge of any node but cannot see the adjacency list wholesale. The paper is
the corpus's pairing exhibit in its purest form: it gives an algorithm, DistWalks, answering
relative-error queries in
$O\bigl(\min\{\log n/\delta,\; m + n\log n\log(m\delta/n)\}\bigr)$ expected queries --- the
first bound linear in $m$ for dense graphs --- and then proves matching lower bounds showing
that, up to logarithmic factors, nothing better exists in this model. Both directions rest on
the same adversary: a family of graphs whose useful information hides in a random classification
that a sublinear number of queries cannot recover.

\paragraph{Problem.}
Fix a directed graph $G$ (multigraphs allowed), source $s$, decay $\alpha$. The PPR value
$\pi(s,t)$ is the probability that an $\alpha$-decay random walk from $s$ (it stops at each
step with probability $\alpha$) ends at $t$. The \emph{SSPPR-R} task: given error
$c \in (0,\tfrac12)$, failure probability $p_f$, and threshold $\delta$, output estimates
$\hat{\pi}(s,\cdot)$ such that with probability at least $1-p_f$,
$|\hat{\pi}(s,t) - \pi(s,t)| \le c\cdot\pi(s,t)$ for every $t$ with $\pi(s,t) \ge \delta$.
The algorithm accesses $G$ through arc-centric queries (the $k$-th in- or out-edge of a named
node); the cost is the expected number of queries. The lower bound may choose any node count
$n_0$, edge count $m_0 \in [n_0, O(n_0^2)]$, and threshold $\delta_0 \in (0,1)$.

\sidenote{DistWalks itself is a concentration showcase --- Monte-Carlo discovery with Chernoff
bounds and martingale error control --- but its \emph{optimality} is an adversarial
construction; the two tool families meet in this paper, and Chapter~\ref{ch:conc} develops the
former. Here we dissect the latter.}

\paragraph{Guarantee.}

\begin{theorem}[Query lower bound for SSPPR-R, Theorem~1.6 of the paper, notation cleaned;
upper bound Theorem~1.4 of the paper]\label{thm:adv-pprlb}
For every $n_0$, every $\delta_0 \in (0,1)$ and every $m_0 \in \Omega(n_0) \cap O(n_0^2)$, any
algorithm solving SSPPR-R with constant error $c$ and constant failure probability $p_f$
requires $\Omega\bigl(\min\{m_0,\; \log(1/\delta_0)/\delta_0\}\bigr)$ expected queries on
graphs with $n_0$ nodes and $m_0$ edges and threshold $\delta_0$. DistWalks answers in
$O\bigl(\min\{\log n/\delta,\; m + n\log n\log(m\delta/n)\}\bigr)$ expected queries ---
matching up to logarithmic factors. \eop
\end{theorem}

Two sibling bounds complete the picture: additive-error estimation requires
$\Omega(\min\{1/\varepsilon_0^2,\, m_0\})$ queries, and the single-target version
$\Omega(\min\{m_0,\, n_0\log n_0/\delta_0\})$ --- the latter by reversing every edge of the
hard family, since an arc-centric algorithm cannot tell a graph from its reverse.

\paragraph{How the adversary is applied.}
The construction runs Yao's principle (Theorem~\ref{thm:adv-yao}) through a \emph{hidden
split}. The template graph $U(n,D,d)$ has a source $s$ feeding a middle layer $Y$ that points
into a target layer $X$ split into two equal classes $X_1, X_2$: by the template, every node of
$X_1$ receives $D$ of its in-edges from the $Y$-layer, every node of $X_2$ only $D-d$. The PPR
values separate exactly at the resolution the task demands --- a calculation gives
$\pi(s,x_1) = \tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\cdot\tfrac{D}{(2D-d)n}$ and
$\pi(s,x_2) = \tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\cdot\tfrac{D-d}{(2D-d)n}$, a ratio $\tfrac{D-d}{D}$ --- so an
estimate within relative error $c$ \emph{classifies every node} of $X$: whoever can estimate
PPR can recover the hidden split. Everything else about the graph is drawn uniformly at random:
the distribution $\Sigma(n,D,d)$ puts a uniform prior on the split and uniform bijections on
all half-edge connections, and an \emph{$r$-padding} (extra edges into dummy node sets) lets
the family be made dense without revealing edges, so the bound covers sparse and dense
regimes alike. The argument then has two moves.

\emph{Move 1 (reduction):} an SSPPR-R algorithm using
$o(\min\{m_0, \log(1/\delta_0)/\delta_0\})$ queries yields, after post-processing its PPR
estimates, a split-recovery procedure using at most $\gamma n D$ arc-centric queries with
average success at least $1 - 2p_f$ (Lemma~4.4 of the paper; the parameters satisfy
$n(r+D) = \Theta(\min\{m_0, \log(1/\delta_0)/\delta_0\})$, so sublinear in the budget means
$o(nD)$).

\emph{Move 2 (indistinguishability):} the split cannot be recovered from $o(nD)$ queries ---
the workhorse lemma.

\begin{lemma}[Switched-split indistinguishability; Lemma~4.6 of the paper, notation cleaned]
\label{lem:adv-switch}
For a sufficiently small constant $\gamma$, after any $T \le \gamma n D$ arc-centric query
answers about $U \sim \Sigma(n,D,d)$ there are $\Omega(n^2)$ \emph{switched splits} ---
splits obtained from the true one by trading the classes of one node of $X_1$ and one node of
$X_2$ --- whose likelihood given the answers is within a factor $n^{1-\varepsilon}$
(of a constant $\varepsilon > 0$) of the true split's likelihood. Consequently every split has
posterior probability less than $1/n$: no procedure outputs the true split with useful
probability. \eop
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Each revealed edge adjacent to a switched node shifts the conditional density by at most
$1 + O(d/D)$ (the $X_1$ versus $X_2$ in-degree discrepancy); all other edges contribute an
exact factor $1$ by the symmetry of the uniform bijections. Few nodes have many revealed edges
(``traversal is hard''), so the product over a switched pair's revealed edges is at most
$(1+O(d/D))^{O(\gamma D)} \le e^{O(\gamma d)} \le n^{1-\varepsilon}$ using $d \le \log n$.
With $\Omega(n^2)$ switched splits each nearly as likely as the truth and a uniform prior,
Bayes crushes the posterior of any single split below $1/n$. (Full proof in
Section~\ref{sec:adv-appendix}.)
\end{proof}

Moves 1 and 2 contradict for large $n$ ($1 - 2p_f$ versus $1/n$), so the assumed fast
SSPPR-R algorithm cannot exist. The two ``hard'' intuitions the paper names ---
\emph{traversal is hard} (sublinear queries leave most nodes' in-edges almost entirely unseen)
and \emph{random walk is hard} (what is unseen is uniformly random, so it carries no class
information) --- are exactly the two-instance pattern sharpened into a likelihood ratio.

\paragraph{What it buys.}
The pairing certifies DistWalks as the end of the line for PPR query complexity in the
arc-centric model, up to logarithmic factors --- a systems-relevant answer, since it tells
engines that the observed cost of PPR service is intrinsic, not an artifact of any
implementation. The construction pattern transfers broadly: any system that must estimate
many values from partially revealed graph structure (adjacency-list services, crawl-based
analysis, API-rate-limited settings) inherits the same hidden-classification adversary.

\section{Advanced usage and further reading}
\label{sec:adv-advanced}

Everything so far plants instances that are \emph{exactly} indistinguishable, counts objects,
or imports conjectures. Two advanced regimes relax the pattern in different directions: the
indistinguishability becomes \emph{computational} (assumed, not exact), and the adversary
attacks a \emph{property} of a design rather than a cost. Each is sketched through one corpus
exemplar.

\subsection{Computational adversaries: indistinguishability by assumption}
\label{sec:adv-comp}

Proposition~\ref{prop:adv-indist} needs transcript distributions that coincide exactly. The
cryptographic weakening asks only that no \emph{efficient} procedure tell the instances apart
--- and then the two-instance pattern survives with efficient adversaries.

\begin{proposition}[Computational two-instance lower bound]\label{prop:adv-comp}
If instances $I_0, I_1$ are indistinguishable to all polynomial-time procedures (no efficient
test separates them with non-negligible advantage) but $f(I_0)$ and $f(I_1)$ differ beyond the
tolerance, then no efficient algorithm computes $f$: an efficient correct algorithm would
itself be a distinguisher. \eop
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Run the supposed algorithm for $f$ on both instances; its answers, which must be accurate,
separate $I_0$ from $I_1$ --- contradicting indistinguishability.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Undetectable DP backdoors \citep{sigmod26-123}.}
The exemplar is a SIGMOD'26 security paper asking whether a malicious vendor can backdoor a
differentially private system without anyone noticing. The \emph{Gaussian pancake mechanism}
(GPM) replaces the Gaussian mechanism's isotropic noise by noise that is Gaussian except for
periodic density spikes --- ``pancakes'' --- concentrated along a secret direction $w$: noise
drawn from the hCLWE distribution. To any polynomial-time auditor without the key $w$, GPM's
outputs are indistinguishable from an honest Gaussian mechanism, because distinguishing hCLWE
from Gaussian is hard under lattice assumptions (SIVP or GapSVP versus quantum computation);
that is Proposition~\ref{prop:adv-comp} with $I_0, I_1$ the two noise distributions, and the
reduction to hCLWE is the imported adversary. Yet with the key, the mechanism is grossly
non-private: privacy loss $\varepsilon \in \Theta(1/\beta^2)$ --- exceeding $10^4$ in practical
regimes --- because neighboring databases shift the pancake alignment along $w$, and the
density ratio between aligned and misaligned peaks is enormous. The paper is the two-instance
pattern \emph{weaponized}: the adversary constructs the indistinguishable pair and profits
from the gap only it can see. Its defensive value is symmetric --- knowing the construction
tells auditors what assumptions they must break or refuse to trust.

\subsection{Adversaries against properties, not costs}
\label{sec:adv-wrecks}

A lower bound certifies optimality; a \emph{counterexample wreck} certifies that a design
principle is unachievable. The smallest adversaries in the corpus are single small graphs that
kill a hoped-for structural property of an objective --- monotonicity, submodularity, the
absence of free riders --- and thereby redirect algorithm design. The exemplar is the
SIGMOD'26 community-search paper LMSC \citep{sigmod26-150}: its objective, local sketch
modularity $\mathrm{LSM}(G,C,\tau) = l_C\,/\,(o_C\cdot|C|^{\tau})$ (internal over external
weight with a size penalty), is shown by explicit clique constructions to be
\emph{non-monotone} and \emph{non-submodular} --- so no greedy algorithm carries approximation
guarantees, and the paper's chain-based relaxation is the response. The same paper's
NP-hardness (a Set-Cover reduction) and its free-rider threshold --- LSM provably resists
query-independent free riders once $\tau > \log_{1+1/h}\!\bigl(h(h-1)/2\bigr)$ for size bound
$h$ --- show the two regimes coexisting: an imported adversary for the complexity statement, a
witness graph of a handful of vertices for the property statements. When your target is a
property rather than a cost, the recipe of Section~\ref{sec:adv-choose} shrinks to: write the
property as an inequality between two small instances, and search for the smallest graph that
violates it.

\subsection{Further reading}

\begin{itemize}
\item Yao \citep{adversarial-construction-bg1} --- the minimax principle turning randomized
  lower bounds into hard distributions; one page, endlessly reused.
\item Kushilevitz and Nisan \citep{adversarial-construction-bg2} --- the two-player adversary
  catalog (index, set-disjointness, gap-Hamming) from which communication-complexity lower
  bounds are imported.
\item Alon, Matias, and Szegedy \citep{adversarial-construction-bg3} --- where sketching lower
  bounds began; the two-instance pattern in stream form.
\item Moon and Moser \citep{adversarial-construction-bg4} --- the $3^{n/3}$ witness and its
  descendants in enumeration optimality.
\item Henzinger, Krinninger, Nanongkai, and Saranurak \citep{adversarial-construction-bg5} ---
  the OuMv conjecture and the reduction-transfer pattern for dynamic problems.
\item Codd \citep{adversarial-construction-bg6} --- the original expressivity question;
  counterexamples measuring relational languages ever since.
\end{itemize}

\begin{recap}
\item \textbf{What the tool is.} Adversarial constructions prove that no better promise exists:
  a counter-world (instance pair, hard distribution, witness object, ceiling-breaking input,
  or imported reduction) in which every procedure permitted by the model fails or pays.
\item \textbf{Which pattern when.} Observation-restricted model $\to$ plant two instances with
  identical transcripts (Proposition~\ref{prop:adv-indist}); randomized algorithms $\to$ harden
  into a distribution via Yao (Theorem~\ref{thm:adv-yao}); cost is the output itself $\to$
  witness counting (Proposition~\ref{prop:adv-output}); target is expressivity $\to$ growth
  ceiling (Proposition~\ref{prop:adv-ceiling}); unrestricted computation $\to$ import a
  conjecture (Proposition~\ref{prop:adv-reduction}); dynamic setting $\to$ two futures of an
  update stream sharing a data-structure state.
\item \textbf{Why it works.} Everything the algorithm does is a function of what it observes;
  two worlds with identical observations but different demands break every algorithm at once.
  The craft is making ``identical'' exact --- or computational
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:adv-comp}) --- while the demanded answers differ beyond tolerance.
\item \textbf{What the corpus showed.} Defective-clique enumeration
  (Section~\ref{sec:adv-defclique}): a Moon--Moser witness generalized by a defect budget,
  certifying worst-case optimality. Proximity graphs (Section~\ref{sec:adv-proxgraph}): a
  tree-like point set forcing $\Omega(n\log\Phi)$ edges regardless of query time, separating
  Euclidean from general doubling metrics. Bi-core maintenance
  (Section~\ref{sec:adv-bicore}): a two-update trap making insertion unbounded for locally
  persistent algorithms while deletion stays bounded. Personalized PageRank
  (Section~\ref{sec:adv-ppr}): a hidden split under a uniform distribution, with likelihood
  ratios over switched splits matching the upper bound up to logarithms --- the corpus's
  signature upper/lower pairing.
\item \textbf{Advanced usage.} Computational indistinguishability turns the two-instance
  pattern into an assumption-backed weapon (the DP backdoor of
  Section~\ref{sec:adv-comp}); counterexample wrecks attack properties of designs rather than
  costs (Section~\ref{sec:adv-wrecks}). Deferred proofs live in the
  Appendix (Section~\ref{sec:adv-appendix}).
\end{recap}

\section{Appendix}
\label{sec:adv-appendix}

Proofs omitted from the main text are collected here.

\subsubsection*{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:adv-mmcount} (Moon--Moser witness count)}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} For $n$ a multiple of $3$ with $n/3 \ge k$, the Moon--Moser
graph $M_n$ (complete $(n/3)$-partite graph, parts of size $3$) contains at least
$3^{n/3}\binom{n/3}{k} = \Omega(3^{n/3} n^k)$ maximal $k$-defective cliques.\eop

\begin{proof}
Write $T_1,\dots,T_{n/3}$ for the parts of $M_n$: vertices in different parts are adjacent;
the $3$ pairs inside a part are non-edges. A set $S \subseteq V$ is $k$-defective exactly when
at most $k$ of the non-edges of $M_n$ lie inside $S$. Construct $S$ as follows: choose a set
$K$ of $k$ parts ($\binom{n/3}{k}$ choices); in each part of $K$ keep both endpoints of one of
its $3$ vertex pairs ($3$ choices each, the kept pair being one missing edge); in each of the
remaining $n/3-k$ parts keep one vertex ($3$ choices each). Then $S$ contains exactly the $k$
non-edges contributed by the parts of $K$ and no others, so $G[S]$ misses exactly $k$ edges:
$S$ is $k$-defective. It is maximal: the only vertices outside $S$ are the third vertices of
parts in $K$, and adding any such vertex $v$ creates a second non-edge inside $v$'s part
($v$ is non-adjacent to both kept vertices of that part), so $S \cup \{v\}$ misses $k+1$
edges and is not $k$-defective. Distinct choices yield distinct sets $S$ (the parts of $K$ are
exactly those contributing two vertices), so the number of maximal $k$-defective cliques is at
least
\[
\binom{n/3}{k}\cdot 3^{k}\cdot 3^{\,n/3-k}
\;=\; \binom{n/3}{k}\cdot 3^{n/3}
\;=\; \Omega\bigl(3^{n/3}\cdot n^{k}\bigr),
\]
where the last step uses $\binom{n/3}{k} = \Omega(n^k)$ for constant $k$. Since every maximal
$k$-defective clique of $M_n$ must be output, Proposition~\ref{prop:adv-output} converts the
count into the enumeration time lower bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:adv-defclique}.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:adv-switch} (switched-split bound)}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} For small enough constant $\gamma$, after any
$T \le \gamma n D$ arc-centric query answers about $U \sim \Sigma(n,D,d)$ there are
$\Omega(n^2)$ switched splits with likelihood within a factor $n^{1-\varepsilon}$ of the true
split's, and every split has posterior probability below $1/n$.\eop

\begin{proof}
Work in the template family of Section~\ref{sec:adv-ppr}: nodes of $X_1$ receive $D$ in-edges
from the $Y$-layer, nodes of $X_2$ receive $D-d$; the split and all half-edge bijections are
uniform. Let $e_1,\dots,e_T$, $T \le \gamma n D$, be the revealed edges and
$\mathrm{SP}$ the true split. \emph{Step 1 (traversal is hard).} Each revealed edge is
incident to at most one node of $X$, so the $T$ edges touch at most $\gamma n D$ in-edge
slots of $X$; by averaging, at least half the nodes of $X$ are incident to at most $2\gamma D$
revealed edges, and because the split is uniform and independent of what queries reveal, both
$X_1$ and $X_2$ contribute $\Omega(n)$ such low-incidence nodes: fix $X_1' \subseteq X_1$,
$X_2' \subseteq X_2$ with $|X_1'|, |X_2'| \ge c n$ and every $x \in X_1' \cup X_2'$ incident
to at most $2\gamma D$ revealed edges. \emph{Step 2 (likelihood ratio per switched pair).}
Fix $x_{i_1} \in X_1'$, $x_{i_2} \in X_2'$ and let $\mathrm{SP}'$ be the switched split. By
Bayes with a uniform prior and the chain rule,
\[
\frac{\Pr[e_1,\dots,e_T \mid \mathrm{SP}]}{\Pr[e_1,\dots,e_T \mid \mathrm{SP}']}
\;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{T}
\frac{\Pr[e_t \mid \mathrm{SP}, e_1,\dots,e_{t-1}]}
     {\Pr[e_t \mid \mathrm{SP}', e_1,\dots,e_{t-1}]}.
\]
For a revealed edge $e_t$ not adjacent to $x_{i_1}$ or $x_{i_2}$, the conditional laws under
the two splits coincide --- the uniform bijections are symmetric in the classes --- and the
factor is exactly $1$. For an edge adjacent to a switched node, the discrepancy between the
$X_1$ in-degree $D$ and the $X_2$ in-degree $D-d$ changes the conditional density of seeing
that particular edge by at most $\tfrac{D}{D-d} = 1 + O(d/D)$. At most $2 \cdot 2\gamma D$
factors differ from $1$, so
\[
\frac{\Pr[e_1,\dots,e_T \mid \mathrm{SP}]}{\Pr[e_1,\dots,e_T \mid \mathrm{SP}']}
\;\le\; \Bigl(1 + O\bigl(\tfrac{d}{D}\bigr)\Bigr)^{O(\gamma D)}
\;\le\; e^{O(\gamma d)}
\;\le\; n^{1-\varepsilon},
\]
using $d \le \log n$ and choosing $\gamma$ a sufficiently small constant (with
$\varepsilon \in (0,1)$ a constant not depending on $n$). \emph{Step 3 (posterior crash).}
The $|X_1'||X_2'| = \Omega(n^2)$ switched splits are distinct and uniform under the prior, so
Bayes gives
\[
\Pr[\mathrm{SP} \mid e_1,\dots,e_T]
\;=\; \frac{\Pr[e_1,\dots,e_T \mid \mathrm{SP}]}
        {\sum_{\mathrm{SP}''}\Pr[e_1,\dots,e_T \mid \mathrm{SP}'']}
\;\le\; \frac{\Pr[e_1,\dots,e_T \mid \mathrm{SP}]}
        {\Omega(n^2)\cdot \Pr[e_1,\dots,e_T \mid \mathrm{SP}]\,/\,n^{1-\varepsilon}}
\;<\; \frac{1}{n}.
\]
The same bound applies to every candidate split --- each is the ``true'' split of some draw,
or dominates an analogous family of switched alternatives --- so any decoder's success
probability is below $1/n$. (Constants follow the paper's appendix; the multigraph templates
are lifted to simple graphs by the paper's 2-lift, which the arc-centric interface cannot
distinguish.)
\end{proof}
