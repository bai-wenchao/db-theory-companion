\begin{objectives}
\item Recognize when a workload admits a small proxy: a query family plus
  a cost to preserve for every query.
\item Prove both ladders: sensitivity sampling $\to$ mergeable
  $\varepsilon$-coresets; random projections $\to$ sketch-and-solve $\to$
  subspace embeddings.
\item Size the proxy by total sensitivity or projection dimension, with
  per-query failure tuned to $\delta/(\text{queries})$ and union-bounded.
\item Merge: distributed shards combine without re-reading the full data.
\end{objectives}

Coresets and randomized numerical linear algebra (RNLA) answer the same
question from two directions. The object is too big to optimize over ---
millions of rows, a dense affinity matrix, a plan space for every query
shape, a join output too large to materialize --- so replace it with a
\emph{small randomized proxy} on which every query of interest is
answered within a $(1\pm\varepsilon)$ factor, with probability
$1-\delta$. The sampling culture (coresets) keeps a subset of the rows
and reweights it, using \emph{sensitivity} scores to decide who must be
kept; the projection culture (sketches) mixes coordinates through a
random matrix, shrinking the object while preserving norms and inner
products. Both pay the same bill --- per-query failure pushed below
$\delta/(\text{queries})$ so one union bound certifies \emph{all} queries
at once --- and both deliver \emph{mergeable} summaries, which is what
makes them the theory engine of distributed and streaming systems.

In the SIGMOD'26 corpus this book surveys, 8 tagged papers use this
machinery --- the survey's coresets-geometry tag (5 papers) merged here
with randomized-nla (3) --- and their pairing is uniform: every exemplar
couples a proxy construction with a probabilistic-guarantee property,
from set-outlier clustering over joins and robust query optimization to
proximity graphs for similarity search. This chapter builds the two
ladders first (Section~\ref{sec:cor-tool}), dissects four exemplars in
full (Sections~\ref{sec:cor-soc}--\ref{sec:cor-pg}), and closes with two
advanced sketches and further reading (Section~\ref{sec:cor-advanced}).

\section{The tool: coresets and randomized NLA}
\label{sec:cor-tool}

The problem class is uniform: a large object $X$ --- point set, matrix,
workload, join output --- and a family $\mathcal{F}$ of \emph{queries},
each $f \in \mathcal{F}$ assigning a nonnegative cost $f(X)$, where
computing on $X$ directly is the bottleneck. The deliverable is a small
randomized $\tilde{X}$ with
\[
\bigl|\, f(\tilde{X}) - f(X) \,\bigr| \;\le\; \varepsilon\, f(X)
\quad\text{for every } f \in \mathcal{F},
\qquad\text{w.p.\ } 1-\delta,
\]
built in near-linear time. Everything here varies three choices:
\emph{what} to keep (rows vs.\ coordinate mixtures), \emph{how} to bias
the choice (sensitivities vs.\ projection distributions), and \emph{how
many} samples or dimensions the union bound demands
(Figure~\ref{fig:cor-proxy}).

\begin{remarkbox}[Notation]
$P = \{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$ is a weighted point set ($w_i$ the weight of
$x_i$); $\mathcal{F}$ a query family; $f(X)$ the answer of query $f$ on
$X$. ``$(1\pm\varepsilon)$-accurate'' means relative error at most
$\varepsilon$; the failure probability is always $\delta$, split across
events by union bounds. On the RNLA side, $R$ is the random sketch
matrix, $\|{\cdot}\|_2$ the Euclidean/operator norm, $\|{\cdot}\|_F$ the
Frobenius norm. For metric exemplars: the \emph{doubling dimension}
$\lambda$ is the least $\lambda$ such that every ball is covered by
$2^\lambda$ balls of half the radius; the \emph{aspect ratio} $\Phi$ is
the diameter divided by the smallest inter-point distance.
\end{remarkbox}

\subsection{The sampling ladder: from uniform to sensitivity}
\label{sec:cor-sampling-ladder}

Uniform sampling is the first idea everyone has, and the first casualty
of skew. Its failure is structural, not an edge case.

\begin{proposition}[Uniform sampling fails on skew]\label{prop:cor-uniform}
For every $n$ and every sample size $s \le n/2$ there is a weighted set
of $n+1$ points in $\mathbb{R}$ such that, with probability at least
$1/2$, a uniform sample of $s$ points --- reweighted by $(n+1)/s$ ---
estimates the weighted cost of one of the two centers $0,1$ as $0$ when
its true cost is $n^2$: no $(1\pm\varepsilon)$ guarantee with constant
$\varepsilon$ is possible. \eop
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Take $p_0$ at the origin with weight $n^2$, and $n$ unit-weight points
at coordinate $1$. For the weighted sum-of-distances cost $f(c) =
\sum_p w_p |p - c|$ we have $f(0) = n$ and $f(1) = n^2$ (the light
points sit exactly at $1$ and contribute $0$ there). The sample misses
$p_0$ with probability $\bigl(1 - \tfrac{1}{n+1}\bigr)^{s} \ge 1 -
\tfrac{s}{n+1} \ge \tfrac12$, and on that event the estimated cost of
center $1$ is $0$ against a true cost of $n^2$.
\end{proof}

Note what failed: not the sample budget but the sampling
\emph{distribution} --- the heavy point above has sensitivity exactly
$1$, yet uniform sampling draws it with probability $\tfrac{1}{n+1}$. The
repair is to sample where the cost concentrates
\citep{coresets-rnla-bg10,coresets-rnla-bg11}, with each point's
worst-case share of a query's cost as the bookkeeping device:

\begin{definition}[Sensitivity]\label{def:cor-sens}
For a weighted set $P$ and a query family $\mathcal{F}$ of cost
functions $f$, the \emph{sensitivity} of point $x \in P$ is
\[
s(x) \;=\; \max_{f \in \mathcal{F}}\ \frac{f(x)}{f(P)},
\]
where $f(x)$ is $x$'s contribution to the answer of $f$. The
\emph{total sensitivity} is $S = \sum_{x \in P} s(x)$.
\end{definition}

Sampling each $x$ with probability proportional to $s(x)$ and
reweighting is the workhorse theorem of the coreset culture. We state
the finite-family version, whose proof is one Hoeffding step plus a
union bound; sharper analyses replace $\ln|\mathcal{F}|$ by the VC-type
complexity of $\mathcal{F}$ \citep{coresets-rnla-bg9,coresets-rnla-bg10}.

\begin{theorem}[Sensitivity sampling]\label{thm:cor-sens}
Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a finite family of nonnegative cost functions on
$P$ with total sensitivity $S \ge 1$, and $\varepsilon, \delta \in
(0,1)$. Draw $m$ i.i.d.\ points $x_1,\dots,x_m$, each equal to $x$ with
probability $s(x)/S$, and give $x_i$ weight $w_i =
\frac{S}{m}\cdot\frac{1}{s(x_i)}$. If $m =
O\bigl(\tfrac{S^2}{\varepsilon^2}
(\ln|\mathcal{F}| + \ln\tfrac{1}{\delta})\bigr)$, then with probability
at least $1-\delta$, simultaneously for every $f \in \mathcal{F}$,
\[
\Bigl|\, \sum_{i=1}^m w_i\, f(x_i) \;-\; f(P) \,\Bigr| \;\le\;
\varepsilon\, f(P). \eopm
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Fix $f$: the weighted sum is a sum of $m$ i.i.d.\ mean-$f(P)$ variables,
each in an interval of length $O\bigl(\tfrac{S}{m} f(P)\bigr)$ because
$f(x) \le s(x) f(P)$ by Definition~\ref{def:cor-sens}. Hoeffding gives
per-$f$ failure $\exp(-\Omega(m\varepsilon^2/S^2))$, and a union bound
over $|\mathcal{F}|$ functions absorbs the $\ln|\mathcal{F}|$ term (full
proof in Section~\ref{sec:cor-appendix}).
\end{proof}

\sidenote{The sensitivity framework is the general theory of importance
sampling for geometric objectives: after a bi-criteria approximation,
the total sensitivity of $k$-median-type objectives is $O(k)$, so
coreset size is $\operatorname{poly}(k, 1/\varepsilon)$ --- independent
of $n$ \citep{coresets-rnla-bg9,coresets-rnla-bg10}. Applied to the rows
of a matrix, the same statistics gives \emph{leverage scores} ---
sensitivity sampling with $f$ ranging over residual directions
\citep{coresets-rnla-bg8}.}

What Theorem~\ref{thm:cor-sens} produces has a name and a closure
property that powers every distributed use.

\begin{definition}[$\varepsilon$-coreset]\label{def:cor-coreset}
A weighted multiset $C$ is an \emph{$\varepsilon$-coreset} of $P$ for
$\mathcal{F}$ if for every $f \in \mathcal{F}$,
$\bigl|\sum_{x \in C} w_x f(x) - f(P)\bigr| \le \varepsilon\, f(P)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Mergeability]\label{lem:cor-merge}
If $C_i$ is an $\varepsilon$-coreset of $P_i$ for $\mathcal{F}$,
$i = 1,2$, then $C_1 \cup C_2$ with summed weights is an
$\varepsilon$-coreset of $P_1 \cup P_2$. \eop
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For every $f$, the two absolute errors add over the disjoint union, and
each is at most $\varepsilon$ times its own part of $f(P_1 \cup P_2)$;
the sum is at most $\varepsilon\, f(P_1 \cup P_2)$.
\end{proof}

Mergeability is why coresets compose across shards of a distributed
database: each shard builds its proxy locally, the proxies are shipped
and unioned, and no shard is read twice. Linear sketches summaries of
the form $RX$ have the same closure by default --- they add across
shards --- which is the design principle behind mergeable streaming
summaries, from AMS sketches \citep{coresets-rnla-bg13} through
CountSketch \citep{coresets-rnla-bg3} to Frequent Directions for
matrices \citep{coresets-rnla-bg12}.

\subsection{The projection ladder: from JL to subspace embeddings}
\label{sec:cor-projection-ladder}

The second culture shrinks the \emph{dimension} rather than the row
count, on the strength of the fact that Euclidean geometry is
compressible: a random low-dimensional projection preserves all pairwise
distances of a fixed point set \citep{coresets-rnla-bg1}.

\begin{theorem}[Johnson--Lindenstrauss]\label{thm:cor-jl}
For every $P = \{p_1,\dots,p_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ and every
$\varepsilon, \delta \in (0,1)$ there is a random linear map
$\Pi\colon \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^{m}$ with
$m = O\bigl(\varepsilon^{-2}(\ln n + \ln\tfrac1\delta)\bigr)$ such
that, with probability at least $1-\delta$, simultaneously for all
pairs $i \ne j$,
\[
(1-\varepsilon)\,\|p_i - p_j\|_2^2 \;\le\;
\|\Pi p_i - \Pi p_j\|_2^2 \;\le\;
(1+\varepsilon)\,\|p_i - p_j\|_2^2. \eopm
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Take $\Pi$ scaled Gaussian. For a \emph{fixed} unit vector $u$,
$\|\Pi u\|_2^2$ is chi-square with an exponential tail --- a
Chernoff-method fact (Chapter~\ref{ch:conc}). Apply this to the
$\binom{n}{2}$ difference directions $u_{ij} = (p_i -
p_j)/\|p_i - p_j\|_2$ with per-pair failure $O(\delta/n^2)$ and
union-bound (full proof in Section~\ref{sec:cor-appendix}).
\end{proof}

\sidenote{The map need not be Gaussian: $\pm1$ signs suffice
\citep{coresets-rnla-bg2}, and sparse sign matrices with one nonzero per
column --- CountSketch \citep{coresets-rnla-bg3} --- make the projection
cost $O(\operatorname{nnz})$ rather than $O(md)$; ``database-friendly''
projections are what make JL deployable inside a scan operator. The
guarantee is also \emph{oblivious}: the same distribution works for
every $P$, so shards share one $R$ and merge by summation.}

JL preserves a \emph{fixed finite set} of directions; numerical linear
algebra needs an entire \emph{subspace} --- every direction in the
column space of a data matrix --- because the optimization variable
ranges over a continuum of candidates
\citep{coresets-rnla-bg4,coresets-rnla-bg6}.

\begin{theorem}[Subspace embedding by sparse sketches]\label{thm:cor-embed}
Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ and $\varepsilon \in (0,1)$. There
is a distribution over CountSketch matrices $R \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times
n}$ with $m = O(d^2/\varepsilon^2)$ rows such that, with constant
probability,
\[
(1-\varepsilon)\,\|Ax\|_2 \;\le\; \|R A x\|_2 \;\le\;
(1+\varepsilon)\,\|A x\|_2
\quad\text{for all } x \in \mathbb{R}^d. \eopm
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
A net argument upgrades a fixed-direction JL guarantee to the whole
$d$-dimensional column space --- preserve a fine net of the sphere and
push the distortion through products --- and the heavy-hitter property
of CountSketch tames the hash collisions; see
\citep{coresets-rnla-bg5,coresets-rnla-bg6} for the complete argument.
\end{proof}

Subspace embeddings convert optimization into \emph{sketch-and-solve}
\citep{coresets-rnla-bg4}: compress, then optimize exactly on the small
instance.

\begin{corollary}[Sketch-and-solve for least squares]\label{cor:cor-sas}
Let $R$ be a $(1\pm\varepsilon)$ subspace embedding for the column
space of $[A \; b]$, where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$, $b \in
\mathbb{R}^n$, and let $\hat{x} = \operatorname{argmin}_{x} \|RAx -
Rb\|_2$. Then
\[
\|A\hat{x} - b\|_2 \;\le\; \frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\,
\min_{x}\|Ax - b\|_2. \eopm
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The embedding preserves \emph{all} residual norms $\|Ax - b\|_2$ at once
(the residuals live in a $(d{+}1)$-dimensional space), so the minimizer
of the preserved objective is near-minimal for the true one (full proof
in Section~\ref{sec:cor-appendix}).
\end{proof}

The last rung, \emph{iterative refinement}, is the practitioner's
caveat about Corollary~\ref{cor:cor-sas}: a $(1\pm\varepsilon)$-embedded
objective solved exactly on the sketch yields only constant-factor
solutions. The RNLA workflow instead uses the sketch as a
\emph{preconditioner} --- solve the sketched system once for a
well-conditioned basis, then a few conjugate-gradient/LSQR iterations on
the \emph{original} residuals, converging at a rate set by the condition
number of that basis \citep{coresets-rnla-bg6,coresets-rnla-bg7,
coresets-rnla-bg14}. Sketch once, refine cheaply.

\subsection{How to choose the proxy}
\label{sec:cor-choose}

The selection is mechanical once two questions are answered. \emph{What
is the query family?} Geometric or combinatorial cost evaluations ---
clustering objectives, covering radii, range-space counts,
plan-switching risks --- point to the sampling track: bound the
sensitivities, sample by them, reweight, size by
Theorem~\ref{thm:cor-sens}. Linear-algebra quantities --- norms, inner
products, regression, low-rank factors, eigenvectors --- point to the
projection track: pick the sketch distribution (Gaussian / sign /
CountSketch), set the dimension by JL or the embedding bound, and
choose sketch-and-solve (Corollary~\ref{cor:cor-sas}) vs.\
precondition-and-refine by the accuracy the downstream consumer needs.
\emph{Where does the object live?} Rows on one machine: either track.
Rows across shards or a stream: insist on mergeability
(Lemma~\ref{lem:cor-merge}; linearity for sketches). Updates and
deletions: linear sketches survive them; row samples generally do not.
Hybrids are the norm --- leverage scores are sensitivities for residual
directions and drive row-sampling regression
\citep{coresets-rnla-bg8}, and the exemplar of
Section~\ref{sec:cor-mrg} sketches an affinity matrix that a geometric
routine balances first.

\begin{reachbox}{When to Reach for Coresets and Sketches}
Reach whenever the cost of a computation is set by the size of an object
that no consumer needs in full --- materializing a join output,
factorizing a dense affinity, enumerating a plan space, scanning every
row per query --- and the analysis tolerates $(1\pm\varepsilon)$ error
with a probability certificate. Then run the recipe:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Identify the query family} $\mathcal{F}$: every quantity
  that must survive the compression, stated as a cost function on the
  object.
\item \textbf{Compute sensitivities or pick the projection}: bound each
  point's worst-case share $s(x)$ of a query (total sensitivity $S$),
  or fix the sketch distribution and dimension; if only leverage scores
  are available, that is the same decision for matrices
  \citep{coresets-rnla-bg8}.
\item \textbf{Verify the $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ guarantee}: per-query
  failure $\exp(-\Omega(\text{sample} \cdot \varepsilon^2/S^2))$ or the
  JL tail; tune the size until it is below
  $\delta/(\text{queries})$, then union-bound over $\mathcal{F}$ (or
  its $\varepsilon$-net).
\item \textbf{Merge if distributed}: union the coresets / sum the
  sketches; by Lemma~\ref{lem:cor-merge} and linearity the guarantee
  survives untouched --- no shard is read twice.
\end{enumerate}
\end{reachbox}

\section{Set-outlier clustering over relational data}
\label{sec:cor-soc}

The exemplar is a SIGMOD'26 paper on clustering with set outliers and
its applications to relational clustering \citep{sigmod26-289}.
Clustering is made robust by allowing the algorithm to discard up to $z$
outliers, but the standard model discards individual \emph{points} ---
the wrong granularity for relational data, where one spurious tuple
pollutes an entire set of join results and burning the outlier budget
point by point leaves the damage in place. The paper introduces
\emph{clustering with set outliers}: exclude up to $z$ sets from a given
family, cluster what remains, and pay only for points no excluded set
covers. For the abstract problem it gives LP-based approximation
algorithms; for hyper-rectangle outlier sets in constant dimension, fast
geometric algorithms; and for outliers induced by the tuples of an
acyclic join, a coreset-based algorithm whose cost is near-linear in the
database size rather than the quadratic-size join output. A
Unique-Games-conditional hardness theorem shows the trade-offs are
nearly tight, and the coreset lens catches the two shrinkage steps
inside: instance reduction, and the relational sample of the join
output.

\paragraph{Problem.}
The abstract problem (Section~2 of \citep{sigmod26-289}, notation
cleaned): given $n$ points $P$ in a metric space, a family
$\mathcal{H}$ of $m$ subsets covering $P$, and integers $k, z$, choose a
center set $C \subseteq P$, $|C| \le k$, and an outlier family
$\mathcal{H}' \subseteq \mathcal{H}$, $|\mathcal{H}'| \le z$, minimizing
the $k$-center-style cost $\mathrm{cost}\bigl(C,\, P \setminus
\bigcup_{h \in \mathcal{H}'} h\bigr)$ --- the maximum distance from any
surviving point to its nearest center. An
$(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$-\emph{approximation} returns a solution of cost
at most $\alpha$ times the optimum using at most $\beta k$ centers and
$\gamma z$ outlier sets. Two refinements matter: the \emph{geometric}
case, $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ with $\mathcal{H}$ a set of $m$
hyper-rectangles covering $P$; and the \emph{relational} case, where $P$
is the output $\Pi(B)$ of an acyclic join over a database $B$ of size
$N$ and each outlier set is the set of join results that use a given
tuple. Hardness: assuming the Unique Games conjecture, no
polynomial-time $(1, f-\varepsilon, \gamma)$-approximation exists for
$f = o(\log n)$ and $\gamma = o(|\mathcal{H}|)$ (Theorem of the paper,
via a Set-Cover reduction encoding set-element incidences as points on a
line and sets as point sets).

\paragraph{Guarantee.}

\begin{theorem}[Set-outlier clustering with hyper-rectangles; Theorem of
\citep{sigmod26-289}, notation cleaned]\label{thm:cor-soc-main}
Given $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ ($d$ constant), $m$ hyper-rectangles
covering $P$, and parameters $k, z, \varepsilon$, there is a
$(2+\varepsilon,\,2,\,O(1))$-approximation algorithm whose running time
is near-linear in $n + m$ once the terms $\beta_1 = \min\{n, km\}$,
$\beta_2 = \min\{n, kz\}$ and $\beta_3 = \min\{m, kz\}$ are isolated
(the dependence on $k$ and $z$ lives in the $\beta$'s). \eop
\end{theorem}

\sidenote{The same LP-plus-rounding skeleton gives a $(2,2,O(1))$%
-approximation for the abstract problem, and --- for bounded doubling
dimension and disjoint outlier sets --- a variant whose running time
drops the $n$ and $m$ terms to the $\beta$'s (Theorem and Corollary of
the paper): once the $\beta$'s are polylogarithmic the algorithm runs
in \emph{sublinear} time. That is the coreset dividend --- the proxy,
not the input, sets the cost.}

\paragraph{How coresets are applied.}
The engine room is a feasibility LP and its rounding; the coreset lens
catches two shrinkage steps. \emph{(i) LP feasibility with
weight-charging rounding.} For a guessed radius $r$, an LP relaxation
encodes ``every point is $r$-covered by center mass or covered by
outlier mass''; if $r \ge r^*_{k,z}$ (the optimum) the optimum itself
witnesses feasibility, and greedy rounding converts feasibility into a
solution with a charging certificate:

\begin{lemma}[Rounding certificate; Lemma of \citep{sigmod26-289},
notation cleaned]\label{lem:cor-soc-charge}
If the feasibility LP is satisfiable for radius $r$, then greedy
rounding returns $C$ and $\mathcal{H}'$ with
$\mathrm{cost}\bigl(C, P \setminus \bigcup_{h\in\mathcal{H}'}
h\bigr) \le 2r$, $|\mathcal{H}'| \le 2fz$ (for LP outlier inflation
$f$), and $|C| \le 2k$. \eop
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Each greedy center is picked where it deactivates all active points
within $2r$, giving the cost bound; the center count comes from
charging to each selected center the LP mass of the $r$-ball around it
--- the balls are disjoint, each carries mass at least $\tfrac12$, and
the total mass is at most $k$ (full proof in
Section~\ref{sec:cor-appendix}).
\end{proof}

\emph{(ii) Instance shrinkage.} The $\beta$ terms in
Theorem~\ref{thm:cor-soc-main} exist because the algorithm first
computes a \emph{sub-instance} --- a coreset of the input itself. For a
feasible radius $r \ge r^*_{k,z}(P,\mathcal{H})$ there are subsets
$P' \subseteq P$ and $\mathcal{H}' \subseteq \mathcal{H}$ with $|P'| =
O(\min\{n, k^2z, km\})$ and $|\mathcal{H}'| = O(\min\{m, kz\})$ such
that the sub-instance's optimum, for adjusted budgets, is at most $18r$
(Lemma of the paper): beyond the sets the optimum already excludes,
only cluster boundaries matter, and $O(k^2z)$ points carry them.
\emph{(iii) The relational coreset.} For an acyclic join with
$|\Pi(B)|$ results, the paper samples points of $\Pi(B)$ through
counting/sampling oracles, builds a BBD-tree over the samples, and
rounds:

\begin{lemma}[Relational coreset; Lemma of \citep{sigmod26-289},
notation cleaned]\label{lem:cor-soc-rel}
Let $Q$ be an acyclic join over a database $B$ of size $N$, and
$\delta = z/|\Pi(B)|$. One can compute $S \subseteq \Pi(B)$ with
$|S| \le k$ and $T \subseteq \Pi(B)$ with $|T| \le (1+\varepsilon)^2
\delta\,|\Pi(B)| = (1+\varepsilon)^2 z$ such that
$\mathrm{cost}\bigl(S, \Pi(B) \setminus T\bigr) \le
3(1+\varepsilon)^2\, r^*_{k,z}\bigl(\Pi(B)\bigr)$, with probability at
least $1 - 1/N$ (via counting/sampling oracles over the join). \eop
\end{lemma}

The probability in Lemma~\ref{lem:cor-soc-rel} is where
\emph{randomized repetition} enters: a single sample catches all tuples
generating the optimal centers with probability only about
$2^{-g(k+z)}$ (each of the $\le g(k+z)$ witness tuples is kept with
probability $\tfrac12$), so the algorithm repeats
$\Theta(2^{g(k+z)} \log N)$ times and unions the per-run failure ---
repetition buying the exponent, exactly the ``push per-event failure
past $\log(\text{events})$'' accounting used throughout this book.
Chaining the coreset with the geometric algorithm yields the paper's
relational theorem: a $(1, 1+\varepsilon, 3+\varepsilon)$-approximation
with probability $1 - 1/N$.

\paragraph{What it buys.}
A query engine can answer ``cluster this join, ignoring damaged
regions'' with cost near-linear in the database size $N$ rather than
the output size $|\Pi(B)|$: the coreset keeps $k$ centers plus
$(1+\varepsilon)^2 z$ excluded results, and everything downstream ---
LP, rounding, binary search --- runs on that proxy. The certified
$(2+\varepsilon,2,O(1))$ trade-off, with UGC hardness showing the
center/outlier budgets cannot all be kept at $1+o(1)$, tells the system
designer exactly which relaxations are free and which are impossible.

\section{Coresets for robust query optimization}
\label{sec:cor-qo}

The exemplar is a PODS'26 paper on coresets for robust query
optimization \citep{sigmod26-053}\pods. Query optimizers pick plans
under cardinality estimates, and estimates are wrong: the plan that
looks best can be catastrophic on the true cardinalities. Robust query
optimization hedges by maintaining a \emph{portfolio} of plans such that
whatever the workload brings, some plan in the portfolio is nearly as
good as the best plan in the entire plan space. Portfolios cost memory
and inference time, so the question is how small one can be. The
paper's answer is a coreset construction in an unusual direction --- the
object compressed is not the data but the \emph{plan space} itself: a
risk-aware portfolio $C \subseteq \Pi$ of size
$O(\kappa^* \log\frac1\delta)$ is computed from only
$O\bigl(\frac{\kappa^*}{\delta^2}\ln N\ln\frac1\delta\bigr)$ sampled
queries, with high probability, where $\kappa^*$ is the best possible
portfolio size. The sizing argument is exactly the recipe of
Section~\ref{sec:cor-choose}: Hoeffding per candidate portfolio, union
bound over all of them. (The greedy selection procedure is dissected
under the exchange-and-greedy lens in Section~\ref{sec:exc-053}; this
section keeps the coreset lens.)

\paragraph{Problem.}
Let $\Pi$ be the set of $N$ candidate plans for a query family, $W$ a
distribution over queries, and each query $Q$ an \emph{uncertainty
profile}: a distribution $U_Q$ over cardinality vectors $s$. The
penalty of plan $\pi$ on $s$ is its cost ratio
$\mathrm{Pnl}(\pi, s) = \mathrm{Cost}(\pi,s)/\mathrm{Cost}^{\star}(s)$
against the optimal plan for $s$, and the \emph{$\tau$-risk} of $\pi$
on $Q$ is $\mathrm{Risk}(\pi, Q;\tau) = \Pr_{s \sim
U_Q}[\mathrm{Pnl}(\pi,s) > \tau]$ --- the probability that $\pi$ blows
past tolerance $\tau$. Writing $\mathrm{Risk}^{\star}(Q;\tau)$ for the
best risk over all of $\Pi$, a set $F \subseteq \Pi$ is an
\emph{$(\varepsilon,\tau,\delta|W)$-coreset for risk} (reconstructed
from Lemmas 3.1 and 3.3 of the paper, notation cleaned) if
\[
\Pr_{Q \sim W}\Bigl[\min_{\pi \in F} \mathrm{Risk}(\pi,Q;\tau) \;>\;
\mathrm{Risk}^{\star}(Q;\tau) + \varepsilon\Bigr] \;\le\; \delta,
\]
i.e., for all but a $\delta$-fraction of the workload some portfolio
plan is within $\varepsilon$ of the best achievable risk; $\kappa^*$ is
the size of the smallest such set.

\paragraph{Guarantee.}

\begin{theorem}[Risk coresets; Theorem 3.5 of \citep{sigmod26-053},
notation cleaned]\label{thm:cor-qo-main}
Let $W$ be a workload distribution, $\Pi$ a set of $N$ plans,
parameters $\varepsilon \in (0,1]$, $\tau \ge 1$, $\delta \in (0,1)$,
and $\kappa^*$ the size of the smallest
$(\varepsilon,\tau,\delta|W)$-coreset for risk. For any fixed
constants $c_1, c_2 > 1$ there is an algorithm that samples
$O\bigl(\tfrac{\kappa^*}{\delta^2}\ln N \ln\tfrac1\delta\bigr)$ queries
from $W$ and computes, in time
$O\bigl(\tfrac{\kappa^*}{\varepsilon^2\delta^2}\,N \ln^3 N
\ln\tfrac1\delta\bigr)$, a set $C \subseteq \Pi$ of size
$O\bigl(\kappa^* \ln\tfrac1\delta\bigr)$ that is, with high
probability, a $(c_1\varepsilon,\,\tau,\,c_2\delta|W)$-coreset for
risk. \eop
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{How coresets are applied.}
The random object is the batch of sampled queries $Q_1, \dots,
Q_{\mathfrak{n}_r} \sim W$ (plus, per query, an inner sample
$s_1,\dots, s_{\mathfrak{m}_r} \sim U_Q$ used to estimate risks); the
query family is, unusually, the family of \emph{all subsets of $\Pi$ up
to size $b_r$} --- every portfolio the algorithm might output is a
query whose cost the sample must certify. Sizing is a two-layer
Hoeffding argument:

\begin{lemma}[Coreset sizing; Lemma 3.1 of \citep{sigmod26-053}, notation
cleaned]\label{lem:cor-qo-hoeffding}
Fix $\varepsilon' \in (0,1)$ and round $r \ge 1$. Let
$\hat{p}_r(\varepsilon', F)$ be the empirical fraction of the
$\mathfrak{n}_r = \tfrac{c_1 k_r}{\delta^2}\ln N \ln\tfrac1\delta$
sampled queries on which $F \subseteq \Pi$ fails to be
$\varepsilon'$-competitive, and $p(\varepsilon',F)$ its expectation.
Then $\hat{p}$ approximates $p$ within $\delta/4$ simultaneously for
all $F \subseteq \Pi$ with $|F| \le b_r = c_3 k_r \ln\frac1\delta$,
with probability at least $1 - N^{-O(1)}$. \eop
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
$\hat{p}_r(\varepsilon',F)$ is a mean of i.i.d.\ $[0,1]$ variables, so
Hoeffding gives failure $2\exp(-\delta^2 \mathfrak{n}_r/8)$ per set;
the number of candidate portfolios is at most $(b_r+1)N^{b_r}$, and a
union bound over them turns the exponent into $N^{-O(1)}$ once $c_1$
dominates $c_3$ (full proof in Section~\ref{sec:cor-appendix}).
\end{proof}

A companion lemma (Lemma 3.2 of the paper) runs the same
Hoeffding-plus-union argument over the $\le N\mathfrak{n}_r$ (plan,
query) pairs for the inner risk estimates, with inner sample size
$\mathfrak{m}_r = \tfrac{c_2}{\varepsilon^2}\ln N \ln
\mathfrak{n}_r$; the $\delta$-budgeting is explicit --- accuracy
$\delta/4$ for the outer fraction, $\varepsilon/10$ for the inner
risks, constants tuned so both failures are polynomially small in every
round. The substitution property --- the reason the proxy can replace
the full plan space at inference time --- is a clean transfer between
\emph{global} risk (against all of $\Pi$) and \emph{local} risk
(against the portfolio):

\begin{lemma}[Risk transfer; Lemma 4.2 of \citep{sigmod26-053}, notation
cleaned]\label{lem:cor-qo-transfer}
For any $F \subseteq \Pi$, $\pi \in F$ and query $Q$:
$\mathrm{Risk}(\pi, Q; \tau^2) \le \mathrm{L\text{-}Risk}_{F}(\pi,
Q;\tau) + \min_{\pi' \in F}\mathrm{Risk}(\pi', Q;\tau)$, where
$\mathrm{L\text{-}Risk}_F$ measures the penalty of $\pi$ relative to
the best plan \emph{in $F$}; consequently $\min_{\pi \in
F}\mathrm{Risk}(\pi,Q;\tau^2) \le 2\min_{\pi\in
F}\mathrm{Risk}(\pi,Q;\tau)$. \eop
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Factor the penalty as a product of the local ratio and the reference
plan's global ratio; the event $\{\mathrm{Pnl} > \tau^2\}$ is then
contained in the union of the two factor events $\{\cdot > \tau\}$ ---
a union bound in disguise (full proof in
Section~\ref{sec:cor-appendix}).
\end{proof}

Chaining Lemma~\ref{lem:cor-qo-transfer} with an empirical version of
the local risk (Hoeffding over the inner samples again) gives the
paper's inference guarantee: the returned plan $\hat{\pi}$ obeys
$\mathrm{Risk}(\hat{\pi},Q;\tau^2) \le
2\,\mathrm{Risk}^{\star}(Q;\tau) + 4\varepsilon$ with probability at
least $1-2\delta$, in $\Theta\bigl(|F|(\ln|F| +
\ln\tfrac1\delta)/\varepsilon^2\bigr)$ time (Theorem 4.1 of the paper):
solving on the proxy costs a factor $2$ --- the standard coreset tax ---
instead of a scan over all $N$ plans.

\paragraph{What it buys.}
The optimizer carries a portfolio of $O(\kappa^*\log\frac1\delta)$
plans instead of $N$, built from a logarithmic number of workload
samples, with a certificate that for all but a $\delta$-fraction of the
workload some portfolio plan is within $\varepsilon$ of the best
achievable risk, and inference against the portfolio runs in time
nearly linear in its size. The sizes adapt to the workload (through
$\kappa^*$) rather than to the plan space, so easy workloads get tiny
portfolios for free.

\section{Sketching the affinity before the eigenvalue work}
\label{sec:cor-mrg}

The exemplar is a SIGMOD'26 paper on effective clustering for large
multi-relational graphs \citep{sigmod26-303}. A multi-relational graph
links its nodes through many relation types at once, and partitioning
the $N$ nodes into $K$ clusters is a standard analytics task that
existing systems either solve inaccurately (crudely fused relations) or
expensively (deep models that stall at millions of nodes). The paper's
pipeline optimizes a normalized-cut-type objective through an affinity
matrix and refuses to ever form the dense $N \times N$ object: the
relaxed problem becomes a $k$-means instance after a balancing step,
the smoothed node embedding behind it is a truncated series of sparse
matrix products, and the Frobenius norms that calibrate everything are
estimated from a CountSketch of the data. Chapter~\ref{ch:spec}
dissects the same paper through the spectral lens (Neumann series,
eigenvalue confinement $\lambda \le 1$, Sinkhorn balancing); this
section keeps the randomized-NLA lens: what is compressed, by which
sketch, and with what probability certificate.

\paragraph{Problem.}
Let $G$ be a multi-relational graph with $N$ nodes and $R$ relations;
relation $r$ induces a (row-normalized) affinity block $S^{(r)}$, fused
into $S = \sum_{r=1}^R \omega_r S^{(r)}$ with $\sum_r \omega_r = 1$.
The clustering objective is a normalized-cut quantity, equivalent to
trace maximization $\max_Y \operatorname{trace}(Y^{\!\top} S\,Y)$ over
node-cluster incidence matrices --- NP-hard, and the standard spectral
relaxation needs a partial eigendecomposition of the \emph{dense}
$N \times N$ matrix $S$. The paper replaces this with a two-stage
program. Stage one computes smoothed node features $Z =
\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\bigl(I - \tfrac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}S\bigr)^{-1} Z^0$
(closed form; the inverse is a Neumann series because the spectrum of
$S$ lies in $[0,1]$ --- Lemma of the paper, proved by Courant--Fischer
plus the Lidskii inequality $\lambda_{\max}(A+B) \le \lambda_{\max}(A)
+ \lambda_{\max}(B)$). Stage two balances $S$ to doubly stochastic by
Sinkhorn--Knopp iterative proportional fitting (row and column
scalings coincide by symmetry and Sinkhorn uniqueness, Theorem of the
paper), which makes the trace objective \emph{equivalent} to weighted
$k$-means on the rows of $Z$. The computational obstacle: evaluating
the objective terms and the truncation error requires Frobenius norms
of dense $N \times N$ products.

\paragraph{Guarantee.}

\begin{corollary}[Sketched objective; Corollary of \citep{sigmod26-303},
notation cleaned]\label{cor:cor-mrg-sketch}
Let $R$ be a CountSketch matrix with
\[
m \;=\; O\Bigl(r\,\varepsilon^{-4}\,
\log\tfrac{r}{\varepsilon\delta}\cdot\bigl(r + \log\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon
\delta}\bigr)\Bigr)
\]
columns, where $r$ is the target rank and $\varepsilon,\delta \in
(0,1)$. Then the sketched estimate of the squared Frobenius norm of
the whitened product $\hat{M}^{(r)} = M\,(M - M^{(r)})^{-1/2}$ --- the
stage-one objective term, whose exact value is the trace quantity
$\operatorname{trace}\bigl(M\,(M-M^{(r)})^{-1}\bigr)$ by the paper's
identity --- is within a $(1\pm\varepsilon)^2$ factor of its exact
value, with probability at least $1-\delta$. \eop
\end{corollary}

\paragraph{How randomized NLA is applied.}
The random object is the CountSketch matrix --- each row of the data is
hashed to one of $m$ buckets with a random sign --- the oblivious
projection of Theorems~\ref{thm:cor-jl} and~\ref{thm:cor-embed}: the
same distribution works for every $M$, so shards sketch independently
and merge by summation. The structure exploited is that every quantity
the pipeline needs is a squared Frobenius norm, and a squared Frobenius
norm is a sum of inner products: the paper first converts its trace
term into $\|\hat{M}^{(r)}\|_F^2$ (identity
$\operatorname{trace}\bigl(M(M-M^{(r)})^{-1}\bigr) =
\|\hat{M}^{(r)}\|_F^2$, Lemma of the paper), then estimates it from
the sketch, because CountSketch preserves pairwise inner products with
high probability --- for every pair of rows, the sketched inner product
is within $\varepsilon$ of the true one with probability $1-\delta$
(the inner-product-preservation property the paper imports). The
$\delta$-budgeting is familiar: per-relation failure $\delta/R$,
union-bounded over the $R$ relations, so the fused estimates inherit
the certificate. Supporting the sketch, the eigenvalue side certifies
that the sketched pipeline is legal: $\lambda(S^{(r)}) \le 1$
(Courant--Fischer plus Lidskii) makes the Neumann series converge, and
truncating after $L$ terms costs at most $\bigl(\tfrac{\alpha}{1+
\alpha}\bigr)^{L+1} \|Z^0\|_F$ divided by the eigengap quantity
$\mu_{L,L+j} = \max_i |\lambda_i(S)^{L+j} - \lambda_i(S)^{L}|$ of the
fused spectrum (Theorem of the paper; the full spectral dissection,
with proofs, is in Section~\ref{sec:spec-mrg}).

\paragraph{What it buys.}
The engine clusters graphs with billions of edges in time linear in
the input instead of quadratic in $N$: no dense $N \times N$ matrix is
ever formed, the embedding is $L$ sparse products with a certified
geometric truncation error, and the calibration norms come from a
mergeable sketch with an $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ certificate. Because
the balancing step makes the objective exactly $k$-means, the final
clustering solves the relaxed problem the sketch was built for --- not
a surrogate of it.

\section{Proximity graphs from covering nets}
\label{sec:cor-pg}

The exemplar is a SIGMOD'26 paper on proximity graphs for similarity
search \citep{sigmod26-349}. Graph indexes are how vector databases
answer nearest-neighbor queries at scale: build a graph over the data
points and answer a query by \emph{greedy routing} --- start anywhere,
repeatedly hop to the neighbor closest to the query, stop when no
neighbor is closer. The graph is a $(1+\varepsilon)$-\emph{proximity
graph} ($(1+\varepsilon)$-PG) if the walk always ends at a
$(1+\varepsilon)$-approximate nearest neighbor; the practical questions
are how many edges it needs, how fast it builds, and how long the walk
takes. The paper settles the trade-off for doubling metrics:
$O(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^\lambda} n\log\Phi)$ edges are enough and
constructible in near-linear time, the $\log\Phi$ is \emph{necessary}
in general (an $\Omega(n\log\Phi)$ lower bound) yet \emph{vanishes} in
constant-dimensional Euclidean space, and the query analysis is a
distance-descent lemma on the greedy walk. The construction itself is
a coreset in metric clothing: a hierarchy of covering nets keeping a
bounded number of representatives per distance scale.

\paragraph{Problem.}
Let $(\mathcal{M}, D)$ be a metric space of doubling dimension
$\lambda$, $P$ an $n$-point subset with aspect ratio $\Phi$, and
$q \notin P$ a query. A directed graph $G$ on $P$ is a
$(1+\varepsilon)$-PG if greedy routing --- from any start, hop to the
out-neighbor closest to $q$, halt when no out-neighbor is closer ---
terminates at a point $p$ with $D(p,q) \le (1+\varepsilon)\,
D(p^{\star},q)$ for an exact nearest neighbor $p^{\star}$. The design
goals: few edges (space), near-linear construction, and short greedy
walks (query time), where the walk length counts distance evaluations.

\paragraph{Guarantee.}

\begin{theorem}[Proximity graphs for doubling and Euclidean metrics;
Theorem of \citep{sigmod26-349}, notation cleaned]\label{thm:cor-pg-main}
(i) For any metric space of constant doubling dimension $\lambda$ and
any $n$-point set $P$ with aspect ratio $\Phi$, there is a
$(1+\varepsilon)$-PG with $O\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^\lambda}\,
n\log\Phi\bigr)$ edges and greedy query time
$O\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^\lambda}\log^2\Phi\bigr)$,
constructible in $O\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^\lambda}\,n\log n\bigr)$
time. (ii) For $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ under $L_2$ with $d = O(1)$,
there is a $(1+\varepsilon)$-PG with only
$O\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^\lambda}\,n\bigr)$ edges and query time
$O\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^\lambda}\log^2\Phi +
\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{d-1}}\log n\,\log^2\Phi\bigr)$; w.h.p.\ it is
built in $O\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^\lambda}\,n\log n\bigr)$ time.
\eop
\end{theorem}

The complementary lower bound shows part (i) cannot lose its
$\log\Phi$:

\begin{theorem}[Size lower bound; Theorem of \citep{sigmod26-349},
notation cleaned]\label{thm:cor-pg-lb}
There is a set of $\Theta(n)$ points with aspect ratio $\Phi$ (a power
of $2$) in a metric space of doubling dimension $1$ such that any
$2$-PG for it must have $\Omega(n\log\Phi)$ edges, \emph{regardless of
the query time allowed}. Also, for every $s \ge 2$, $t \ge 1$,
$d \ge 1$ there is an $n = s^d \cdot t$ point set with doubling
dimension at most $\log(1+2^d)$ such that any
$(1+\tfrac{1}{2s})$-PG needs $\Omega(s^d \cdot n)$ edges. \eop
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{How coresets are applied.}
The proxy is a \emph{covering-net hierarchy}: nested subsets $Y_h
\subseteq \dots \subseteq Y_1 \subseteq Y_0 = P$ where $Y_i$ is a
$2^i$-net --- every point of $P$ is within $2^i$ of some point of
$Y_i$ --- and $h = \log\Phi$ indexes the scales from the minimum
inter-point distance to the diameter. The graph keeps, for each point,
edges to nearby net representatives at every scale; the decomposition
is a coreset of the metric --- a per-scale sample of the point set that
preserves every point's distance profile to within the scale
resolution --- and its sparsity is what sizes the graph.

\begin{lemma}[Net out-neighbors; from the construction and its proof in
\citep{sigmod26-349}, notation cleaned]\label{lem:cor-pg-net}
In the covering-net construction, every point $p \in P$ keeps, at each
scale $i$, an out-neighbor $y \in Y_i$ with $D(p,y) \le 2^i$ (the
covering property); the per-point out-degree is
$O\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^\lambda}\log\Phi\bigr)$, and the graph
has $O\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^\lambda} n\log\Phi\bigr)$ edges.
\eop
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Covering supplies the per-scale representative; the doubling property
bounds how many net points matter in $p$'s distance window at each
scale, and there are $O(\log\Phi)$ scales (full proof in
Section~\ref{sec:cor-appendix}).
\end{proof}

The query analysis is where the paper's sharpest lemma lives --- the
greedy walk descends the net hierarchy, and each ``jackpot'' hop
shrinks the \emph{logarithm} of the distance to the true nearest
neighbor:

\begin{lemma}[Greedy descent and log-drop; Lemma of \citep{sigmod26-349},
notation cleaned]\label{lem:cor-pg-drop}
Fix a query $q$ and $p \in P$ that is not a $(1+\varepsilon)$-ANN of
$q$; let $p^+$ be the out-neighbor of $p$ closest to $q$. Then (i)
$D(p^+, q) < D(p, q)$; and (ii) for any $x \in P$ with $D(x,q) \le
D(p^+,q)$ that is not a $(1+\varepsilon)$-ANN of $q$,
\[
\log D(x, p^{\star}) \;<\; \log D(p, p^{\star})
\]
for an exact nearest neighbor $p^{\star}$ of $q$. \eop
\end{lemma}

Statement (ii) is the workhorse: between two greedy-descent
configurations the (log) distance to the nearest neighbor strictly
drops, so after $O(\log\Phi)$ drops the walk is within scale $1$ of
$p^{\star}$, and each inter-drop segment is short because the walk is
monotone in distance to $q$ while the net hierarchy has only
$O(\log\Phi)$ scales (the paper splits the walk into subsequences at
jackpot hops and charges each subsequence $\Theta(\log n\,\log\Phi)$
hops). The Euclidean separation of Theorem~\ref{thm:cor-pg-main}(ii)
removes one $\log\Phi$ from the \emph{space}: in $L_2$, an
angle-separation lemma (points equidistant from $p$ on nested query
spheres are separated by angle $> \pi/8$) shows each scale needs only
a bounded cone of representatives per point, while the adversarial
metrics of Theorem~\ref{thm:cor-pg-lb} --- recursively nested clusters
--- prove general doubling metrics enjoy no such savings.

\paragraph{What it buys.}
The vector-search index gets graphs that are simultaneously sparse
(affordable memory), near-linearly constructible (affordable builds),
and certifiably correct under greedy routing with polylogarithmic query
paths --- and the matching lower bound tells the engineer that pushing
space below $n\log\Phi$ in general doubling metrics is provably
impossible, so optimization effort belongs in the Euclidean regime
where the extra $\log\Phi$ genuinely vanishes.

\section{Advanced usage and further reading}
\label{sec:cor-advanced}

The four exemplars share one pattern: a coreset or sketch whose size is
governed by complexity parameters ($k, z$; $\kappa^*$; rank $r$;
doubling dimension and $\Phi$) rather than by the data size. Two
further corpus papers use the same machinery in harder combinations;
each is sketched in one paragraph.

\paragraph{Spanners as distance-preserving proxies on terrains
\citep{sigmod26-052}.}
The constrained shortest path problem CPTS asks for a shortest path on
a terrain surface that visits a set of points of interest, and is
NP-hard by reduction from Euclidean TSP (Theorem 3.3 of the paper).
The proxy is a \emph{spanner}: a sparse graph $G$ over the terminal
points with $d_g(u,v) \le d_G(u,v) \le (1+\varepsilon)\,d_g(u,v)$ for
all pairs, where $d_g$ is the geodesic distance --- exactly the coreset
contract of Definition~\ref{def:cor-coreset} with query family
$\mathcal{F} = \{\text{pairwise distances}\}$, satisfied for
\emph{every query at once} by a deterministic construction. The spanner
lemmas are proved by contradiction and induction on an iterative
geometric procedure (with triangle-inequality charging of the
per-iteration weights), and the final route is assembled on $G$ by
Kruskal's MST plus a pre-order traversal, whose edge-weight accounting
yields a factor-$2$ path over the spanner --- solve on the proxy, lift
back with a certificate, the signature move of this chapter in a
deterministic register.

\paragraph{Sketches inside a privacy budget \citep{sigmod26-127}.}
HeteroFedSyn synthesizes tabular data under differential privacy across
heterogeneous participants. Its privacy accounting is zCDP composition
(mechanisms with parameters $\rho_i$ compose to $\rho = \sum_i
\rho_i$, Definition of the paper) plus post-processing, which turns
budget allocation across the $O(d^2)$ two-way marginals into an
optimization problem; and its compression is this chapter's: a random
projection squeezes the two-way marginal vectors before the Gaussian
noisy release, so the noise (calibrated to the projected sensitivity)
covers fewer coordinates. An exact expectation calculation strips the
projection and noise bias, yielding an unbiased estimator of the
pairwise dependency measure $\mathrm{InDif}^2$ (Theorem of the paper),
and a greedy selection spends the budget where dependency error
dominates noise error. The RNLA lesson: inside a privacy pipeline the
sketch is not an optimization but a \emph{variance} device --- fewer
released coordinates means less noise per coordinate at fixed $\rho$.

\subsection{Further reading}

\begin{itemize}
\item Coresets: the unified sensitivity framework of Feldman and
  Langberg \citep{coresets-rnla-bg10}; the $k$-means/$k$-median
  construction of Har-Peled and Mazumdar \citep{coresets-rnla-bg9};
  the geometric survey of Agarwal, Har-Peled, and Varadarajan
  \citep{coresets-rnla-bg11}.
\item Random projections: the original Johnson--Lindenstrauss lemma
  \citep{coresets-rnla-bg1} and the database-friendly $\pm1$ variant
  of Achlioptas \citep{coresets-rnla-bg2}.
\item Sketching for linear algebra: Sarl\'os's projection-based
  regression \citep{coresets-rnla-bg4}, input-sparsity subspace
  embeddings of Clarkson and Woodruff \citep{coresets-rnla-bg5},
  leverage-score sampling \citep{coresets-rnla-bg8}, and Woodruff's
  monograph \citep{coresets-rnla-bg6}.
\item The randomized-NLA survey of Mahoney
  \citep{coresets-rnla-bg7} and the randomized matrix-decomposition
  treatment of Halko, Martinsson, and Tropp
  \citep{coresets-rnla-bg14} cover the iterative-refinement mode of
  Section~\ref{sec:cor-projection-ladder}.
\item Mergeable summaries: AMS frequency-moment sketches
  \citep{coresets-rnla-bg13}, CountSketch
  \citep{coresets-rnla-bg3}, and Frequent Directions for matrix
  sketches \citep{coresets-rnla-bg12}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{recap}
\item \textbf{What the tool is.} A small randomized proxy --- a
  weighted row sample (coreset) or a random coordinate mixture
  (sketch) --- that answers every query in a family within
  $(1\pm\varepsilon)$ with probability $1-\delta$, built in
  near-linear time and sized by complexity parameters, not data size.
\item \textbf{Which track when.} Geometric or combinatorial cost
  queries: sensitivity sampling (Theorem~\ref{thm:cor-sens}) ---
  uniform sampling fails on skew
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:cor-uniform}). Linear-algebra queries: JL for
  finitely many directions (Theorem~\ref{thm:cor-jl}), subspace
  embeddings for a continuum of candidates
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:cor-embed}); solve on the sketch for constant
  factors (Corollary~\ref{cor:cor-sas}), refine iteratively for high
  accuracy. Distributed or streaming: insist on mergeability
  (Lemma~\ref{lem:cor-merge}; linearity for sketches).
\item \textbf{Why it works.} Per-query failure is pushed below
  $\delta/(\text{queries})$ --- Hoeffding on the weighted sample, or
  the chi-square tail on the projection --- and one union bound (over
  the query family, an $\varepsilon$-net, or all candidate portfolios)
  certifies every query simultaneously.
\item \textbf{What the corpus showed.} Set-outlier clustering
  (Section~\ref{sec:cor-soc}): LP-guided instance shrinkage to
  $O(k^2z)$ points and a $k + (1+\varepsilon)^2 z$-result coreset of
  the join output, with randomized repetition over samples. Robust
  query optimization (Section~\ref{sec:cor-qo}): Hoeffding-plus-union
  sizing of plan portfolios, with global-to-local risk transfer as the
  substitution property. Multi-relational clustering
  (Section~\ref{sec:cor-mrg}): CountSketch estimates the objective
  norms so the dense affinity is never formed. Proximity graphs
  (Section~\ref{sec:cor-pg}): covering-net hierarchies (metric
  coresets) with a log-drop lemma bounding greedy query paths, plus
  $\Omega(n\log\Phi)$ optimality.
\item \textbf{Advanced usage.} Deterministic distance proxies
  (spanners) satisfy the coreset contract without randomness; sketches
  inside privacy pipelines trade coordinates against noise at fixed
  budget (Section~\ref{sec:cor-advanced}). Deferred proofs live in the
  Appendix (Section~\ref{sec:cor-appendix}).
\end{recap}

Proofs omitted from the main text are collected here.

\subsubsection*{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:cor-sens} (sensitivity sampling)}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} Draw $m$ i.i.d.\ points, each $x$ with
probability $s(x)/S$, weight $w_i = \tfrac{S}{m}\,\tfrac{1}{s(x_i)}$,
with $m = \Theta\bigl(\tfrac{S^2}{\varepsilon^2}(\ln|\mathcal{F}| +
\ln\tfrac1\delta)\bigr)$; then w.h.p.\ every $f \in \mathcal{F}$ has
$|\sum_i w_i f(x_i) - f(P)| \le \varepsilon f(P)$.\eop

\begin{proof}
Fix $f$ and let $Z_i = w_i f(x_i)$. Each draw picks $x$ with
probability $p_x = s(x)/S$, so $\mathbb{E}[Z_i] = \sum_x
p_x\,\tfrac{S}{m s(x)} f(x) = \tfrac{1}{m}\sum_x f(x) =
\tfrac{f(P)}{m}$ --- the estimator is unbiased. For the range, $f(x)
\le s(x) f(P)$ (Definition~\ref{def:cor-sens}) gives $0 \le Z_i \le
\tfrac{S}{m}\,f(P)$, so the $Z_i$ are i.i.d.\ in an interval of length
$b = \tfrac{S}{m}f(P)$. Hoeffding (Theorem~\ref{thm:conc-hoeffding})
yields
\begin{align*}
\Pr\Bigl[\Bigl|\sum_i Z_i - f(P)\Bigr| \ge \varepsilon f(P)\Bigr]
&\;\le\; 2\exp\Bigl(-\,\frac{2m^2\varepsilon^2 f(P)^2}{m b^2}\Bigr)\\
&\;=\; 2\exp\bigl(-2m\varepsilon^2/S^2\bigr).
\end{align*}
With $m \ge \tfrac{S^2}{2\varepsilon^2}\bigl(\ln|\mathcal{F}| +
\ln\tfrac{2}{\delta}\bigr)$ this is at most $\delta/|\mathcal{F}|$ per
query, and a union bound over all $f \in \mathcal{F}$ completes the
proof.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:cor-jl} (Johnson--Lindenstrauss)}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} A scaled Gaussian map $\Pi \in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}$ with $m = O(\varepsilon^{-2}(\ln n +
\ln\tfrac1\delta))$ preserves all $\binom{n}{2}$ pairwise squared
distances of $P$ to $(1\pm\varepsilon)$ with probability $\ge
1-\delta$.\eop

\begin{proof}
Let $\Pi = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{m}}G$ with $G_{ij} \sim N(0,1)$ i.i.d.\ For
a fixed unit vector $u$, $Y_u = \|\Pi u\|_2^2 = \tfrac1m\sum_{j=1}^m
g_j^2$ with $g_j \sim N(0,1)$ i.i.d. The Chernoff method
(Lemma~\ref{lem:conc-chernoff-method}) applied to
$\mathbb{E}\,e^{\lambda g^2} = (1-2\lambda)^{-1/2}$, optimized over
$\lambda$, gives the chi-square tail: for $\varepsilon \in (0,1)$,
\[
\Pr\bigl[\,|Y_u - 1| > \varepsilon\,\bigr] \;\le\;
2\,e^{-m\varepsilon^2/12}.
\]
Apply this to each difference direction $u_{ij} = (p_i -
p_j)/\|p_i-p_j\|_2$: since $\Pi$ is linear, $\|\Pi p_i - \Pi
p_j\|_2^2 = \|p_i - p_j\|_2^2\,Y_{u_{ij}}$, so
$(1\pm\varepsilon)$-preservation of $Y_{u_{ij}}$ is preservation of
the pair. Union-bounding over the $\binom{n}{2} \le n^2/2$ pairs, the
total failure probability is at most $n^2 e^{-m\varepsilon^2/12} \le
\delta$ for the stated $m$.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:cor-sas} (sketch-and-solve)}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} If $R$ is a $(1\pm\varepsilon)$
subspace embedding for the column space of $[A\;b]$ and $\hat{x}$
minimizes $\|RAx - Rb\|_2$, then $\|A\hat{x}-b\|_2 \le
\tfrac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\min_x \|Ax - b\|_2$.\eop

\begin{proof}
Let $x^{\star}$ minimize $\|Ax - b\|_2$. The embedding hypothesis
holds for \emph{every} $x$: since $Ax - b$ lies in the column space of
$[A\;b]$,
\begin{align*}
(1-\varepsilon)\,\|A\hat{x}-b\|_2
&\;\le\; \|RA\hat{x}-Rb\|_2 \;\le\;
\|RAx^{\star}-Rb\|_2\\
&\;\le\; (1+\varepsilon)\|Ax^{\star}-b\|_2,
\end{align*}
where the middle inequality uses that $\hat{x}$ minimizes the sketched
objective. Dividing by $1-\varepsilon$ gives the claim.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:cor-qo-hoeffding} (coreset sizing)}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} The empirical failure frequency
$\hat{p}_r(\varepsilon',F)$ over $\mathfrak{n}_r =
\tfrac{c_1k_r}{\delta^2}\ln N\ln\tfrac1\delta$ i.i.d.\ queries
approximates $p(\varepsilon',F)$ within $\delta/4$, simultaneously for
all $|F| \le b_r = c_3k_r\ln\tfrac1\delta$, with probability
$1 - N^{-O(1)}$.\eop

\begin{proof}
For fixed $F$, $\hat{p}_r(\varepsilon',F)$ is a mean of
$\mathfrak{n}_r$ i.i.d.\ $[0,1]$ indicators with expectation
$p(\varepsilon',F)$, so Hoeffding gives
\begin{align*}
\Pr\bigl[\,|\hat{p}_r(\varepsilon',F) - p(\varepsilon',F)| >
\delta/4\,\bigr] &\;\le\; 2\exp\bigl(-2\mathfrak{n}_r(\delta/4)^2\bigr)\\
&\;=\; 2\exp\bigl(-\delta^2\mathfrak{n}_r/8\bigr).
\end{align*}
The number of subsets of $\Pi$ of size at most $b_r$ is at most
$(b_r+1)N^{b_r}$, so the probability that \emph{any} $F$ violates the
accuracy is at most $2(b_r+1)N^{b_r}\exp(-\delta^2\mathfrak{n}_r/8)$.
Substituting $\mathfrak{n}_r = \tfrac{c_1k_r}{\delta^2}\ln
N\ln\tfrac1\delta$ and $b_r = c_3k_r\ln\tfrac1\delta$, the exponent
$\delta^2\mathfrak{n}_r/8 - b_r\ln N$ is $\Theta(c_1 k_r \ln N
\ln\tfrac1\delta) - \Theta(c_3 k_r \ln\tfrac1\delta\ln N)$, which is
$\Theta(c_1 k_r \ln N \ln\tfrac1\delta)$ once $c_1$ dominates $c_3$
--- so the bound is $N^{-O(1)}$ as claimed (proof transcribed from
Lemma 3.1 of \citep{sigmod26-053}).
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:cor-qo-transfer} (risk transfer)}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} For $F \subseteq \Pi$, $\pi \in F$, and query $Q$,
\[
\mathrm{Risk}(\pi,Q;\tau^2) \;\le\; \mathrm{L\text{-}Risk}_F(\pi,Q;\tau) +
\min_{\pi'\in F}\mathrm{Risk}(\pi',Q;\tau),
\]
hence $\min_{\pi\in F}\mathrm{Risk}(\pi,Q;\tau^2) \le
2\min_{\pi\in F}\mathrm{Risk}(\pi,Q;\tau)$.\eop

\begin{proof}
Fix $s$ and any $\pi' \in F$. Since the best cost in $F$ is at least
the best cost overall,
\[
\mathrm{Pnl}(\pi,s) \;=\;
\frac{\mathrm{Cost}(\pi,s)}{\mathrm{Cost}(\pi',s)}\cdot
\frac{\mathrm{Cost}(\pi',s)}{\mathrm{Cost}^{\star}(s)} \;\le\;
\mathrm{L\text{-}Pnl}_F(\pi,s)\cdot \mathrm{Pnl}(\pi',s).
\]
Define $E_1 = \{s : \mathrm{Pnl}(\pi,s) > \tau^2\}$, $E_2 = \{s :
\mathrm{L\text{-}Pnl}_F(\pi,s) > \tau\}$, $E_3 = \{s :
\mathrm{Pnl}(\pi',s) > \tau\}$. If $s \in E_1$ then the product bound
forces $s \in E_2 \cup E_3$ (both factors $\le \tau$ would give
$\mathrm{Pnl} \le \tau^2$). Hence, for $s \sim U_Q$,
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{Risk}(\pi,Q;\tau^2) = \Pr[E_1] &\le \Pr[E_2] + \Pr[E_3]\\
&= \mathrm{L\text{-}Risk}_F(\pi,Q;\tau) + \mathrm{Risk}(\pi',Q;\tau),
\end{align*}
and minimizing the last term over $\pi' \in F$ gives the first claim.
Applying it to a minimizer $\pi^{\star}_F$ of the $\tau$-risk over
$F$, and using that the local penalty never exceeds the global one
($\mathrm{L\text{-}Risk}_F(\pi^{\star}_F,Q;\tau) \le
\mathrm{Risk}(\pi^{\star}_F,Q;\tau)$), yields the factor-$2$ form
(proof transcribed from Lemma 4.2 of \citep{sigmod26-053}).
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:cor-soc-charge} (weight charging)}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} If the feasibility LP for radius $r$
is satisfiable, greedy rounding returns $C, \mathcal{H}'$ with cost
$\le 2r$, $|\mathcal{H}'| \le 2fz$ and $|C| \le 2k$.\eop

\begin{proof}
\emph{Cost:} the algorithm stops only when no active points remain,
and each selected center deactivates every active point within $2r$;
hence all surviving points are within $2r$ of $C$. \emph{Outliers:} if
more than $2fz$ sets were chosen, the corresponding LP variables would
each exceed $\tfrac{1}{2f}$ and sum beyond $z$ --- contradicting the
LP constraint. \emph{Centers:} charge to each selected center $p_i$
the LP mass $w_{p_i} = \sum_{p \in B(p_i,r)} x^{\star}_p$ of the
$r$-ball around it. For a point $p_i$ not covered by chosen outlier
sets, the LP forces $\sum_{p \in B(p_i,r)} x^{\star}_p \ge
\tfrac12$ (its outlier variables sum to at most $\tfrac12$, so the
covering constraint must be met by center mass), so $w_{p_i} \ge
\tfrac12$. The balls of selected centers are pairwise disjoint (each
selection deactivates everything within $2r$, so two centers are more
than $2r$ apart), the charges do not overlap, and $\sum_i w_{p_i} \le
\sum_p x^{\star}_p \le k$; with each charge at least $\tfrac12$,
$|C| \le 2k$. (Under the $\varepsilon$-approximate range queries the
relational algorithm uses, the charge degrades to $\tfrac12 -
\varepsilon$ and the bound to $2k/(1-2\varepsilon)$.) Proof
transcribed from the rounding lemma of \citep{sigmod26-289}.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:cor-pg-net} (net out-neighbors)}
{\itshape Statement (recalled).} Every $p \in P$ keeps, at each scale
$i$, an out-neighbor $y \in Y_i$ with $D(p,y) \le 2^i$; per-point
out-degree is $O(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^\lambda}\log\Phi)$.\eop

\begin{proof}
Fix $p$ and scale $i$. By the covering property of the net $Y_i$
there is $y \in Y_i$ with $D(p,y) \le 2^i$, and the construction
links $p$ to such $y$ --- routing to $y$ makes progress at scale $i$
(the covering step the paper's construction proof verifies). There
are $h = O(\log\Phi)$ scales from the minimum inter-point distance to
the diameter, and the doubling property bounds the number of relevant
net points in $p$'s distance window at each scale by $O(2^\lambda)$,
taken over $(1+\varepsilon)$-shells for the accuracy parameter,
giving the stated per-point degree (argument follows the construction
proof of \citep{sigmod26-349}).
\end{proof}
